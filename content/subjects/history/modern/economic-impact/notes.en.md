# Economic Impact of British Rule

## Overview {#overview}

British rule changed the Indian economy from a largely self-sufficient agrarian and handicraft economy into a supplier of raw materials and a market for British manufactured goods. The turning point was the grant of the **Diwani** (the right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa to the East India Company by the **Treaty of Allahabad (1765)**. From then on, land revenue became the Company's main source of income, and much of it financed its trade and wars.

Historians such as **Bipan Chandra** describe colonial exploitation in three broad, overlapping stages:

| Stage | Approximate period | Main feature |
|---|---|---|
| Mercantile | 1757–1813 | Company's trade monopoly; Indian goods bought with Indian revenue |
| Free trade (industrial capital) | 1813–1858 | India opened to British manufactures; demand for raw materials |
| Finance imperialism | from about 1860 | British capital invested in railways, plantations, banks, loans to the Government of India |

The main channels of impact studied for exams are:
- new **land revenue systems** (Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, Mahalwari);
- the **drain of wealth** from India to Britain;
- **deindustrialisation**, i.e. the ruin of traditional handicrafts;
- **commercialisation of agriculture**;
- **railways** built mainly to serve British trade and administration;
- recurring **famines** and the government's slow response to them.

:::keyfacts
- Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, Orissa: **1765**, Treaty of Allahabad
- Company's trade monopoly in India ended: **Charter Act of 1813**
- Earliest systematic critique of colonial economics came from the **early nationalists** (Dadabhai Naoroji, R.C. Dutt, M.G. Ranade)
:::

## Permanent Settlement {#permanent-settlement}

After the Diwani, the Company experimented with several ways of collecting revenue in Bengal. Under **Warren Hastings**, the right to collect revenue was auctioned to the highest bidder for five years (**Panchsala settlement, 1772**), and later annually. Revenue was uncertain and many cultivators were ruined. A **ten-year (decennial) settlement** was made in 1790, and it was declared permanent in 1793.

### Main features
- Introduced by **Lord Cornwallis** in **1793**, in **Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** (later extended to the Banaras division and parts of northern Madras). **John Shore** helped plan it.
- The **zamindars** were recognised as **owners of the land**, with the right to sell, mortgage or transfer it.
- The amount of revenue the zamindar had to pay to the Company was **fixed permanently**; it could never be raised.
- The zamindar collected rent from the peasants and kept a small share; at the time of the settlement the bulk (about **ten-elevenths** of the assessed rental) went to the government.
- **Sunset Law**: the fixed revenue had to be paid by **sunset on the due date**. If the zamindar failed, his estate (or part of it) was **sold by auction**.

### Aims
- A **fixed, assured income** for the Company.
- A loyal class of landowners who would support British rule.
- The hope that secure owners would invest in improving land.

### Effects
- Many old zamindari families lost their estates under the Sunset Law; their lands were bought by merchants and other new buyers.
- **Absentee landlordism** and many layers of intermediaries (subinfeudation) grew between the zamindar and the cultivator.
- The **peasants (ryots)** got no security of tenure and were often forced to pay high rents.
- As agriculture expanded, the zamindars' rental income rose, but the **government's share stayed fixed**, so the state lost potential revenue in the long run.

:::caution
The Permanent Settlement fixed the revenue that the **zamindar paid to the state**. It did **not** fix the rent that the **peasant paid to the zamindar**.
:::

## Ryotwari and Mahalwari Systems {#ryotwari-mahalwari}

Because the Permanent Settlement froze the state's income, later settlements were **temporary**: revenue was revised periodically.

### Ryotwari system
- Associated with **Alexander Read** and **Thomas Munro**. Read first tried it in the **Baramahal** region; Munro extended it in the **Madras Presidency** (he became Governor of Madras in 1820).
- Later extended to the **Bombay Presidency**, and to parts of Assam and Coorg.
- The settlement was made **directly with each cultivator (ryot)**, who was recognised as the owner of the land so long as he paid the revenue.
- No intermediary such as a zamindar; the **state itself became the landlord** in practice.
- Revenue rates were high and **revised periodically**, which left the ryots exposed to moneylenders.

### Mahalwari system
- Proposed by **Holt Mackenzie** in **1822**; revised under **Lord William Bentinck** in 1833, with **R.M. Bird** playing a major role.
- Introduced in the **North-Western Provinces** (Ganga valley), parts of central India and **Punjab**.
- The unit of settlement was the **mahal** (village or estate). The **village community jointly** was responsible for paying the revenue; the village headman (**lambardar**) collected it.
- Revenue was **revised periodically**.

### Comparison

| Feature | Permanent Settlement | Ryotwari | Mahalwari |
|---|---|---|---|
| Associated with | Lord Cornwallis (1793) | Alexander Read, Thomas Munro | Holt Mackenzie (1822) |
| Main areas | Bengal, Bihar, Orissa | Madras, Bombay, parts of Assam, Coorg | North-Western Provinces, Punjab, parts of central India |
| Settlement made with | Zamindar | Individual cultivator (ryot) | Village community (mahal) |
| Revenue demand | Fixed permanently | Revised periodically | Revised periodically |
| Approx. share of British Indian area (as given in standard texts) | about 19% | about 51% | about 30% |

:::mnemonic
"**C**ornwallis **Z**ooms, **M**unro **R**ides, **M**ackenzie **M**arches": Cornwallis–Zamindar, Munro–Ryot, Mackenzie–Mahal.
:::

:::exam-tip
Remember the **unit of settlement**: zamindar (Permanent), individual ryot (Ryotwari), village community (Mahalwari). Questions often swap these.
:::

## Drain of Wealth {#drain-of-wealth}

The **drain of wealth** refers to the transfer of a part of India's national product to Britain without any economic or material return to India. The early nationalists made it the centre of their economic critique of British rule.

### Main thinkers
- **Dadabhai Naoroji**, known as the "Grand Old Man of India", developed the drain theory most fully. His book **"Poverty and Un-British Rule in India" (1901)** argued that India's poverty was the result of this drain. He was elected to the **British House of Commons in 1892**.
- **R.C. Dutt** (Romesh Chunder Dutt) wrote **"The Economic History of India"**, published in two volumes in the early 1900s. He criticised heavy land revenue and the ruin of Indian industries. He presided over the **Indian National Congress session of 1899 (Lucknow)**.
- **M.G. Ranade** and others also wrote on the drain and on India's economic backwardness.
- The later study **"The Rise and Growth of Economic Nationalism in India"** by **Bipan Chandra** examines the economic ideas of these early nationalists.

### Components of the drain
- **Home Charges**: expenses paid in Britain on behalf of the Government of India, such as salaries and pensions of British officials, expenses of the India Office in London, and interest on the public debt raised in Britain.
- **Interest on foreign capital**, including the guaranteed interest on railway investments.
- **Profits and savings** sent home by British merchants, officials and companies.
- Cost of the army, including wars fought outside India, charged to Indian revenues.

### Significance
- The drain reduced the capital available for investment inside India.
- The drain theory exposed the exploitative nature of colonial rule and laid the base of **economic nationalism**.

:::caution
"Poverty and Un-British Rule in India" is by **Dadabhai Naoroji**; "The Economic History of India" is by **R.C. Dutt**. Do not mix them up.
:::

## Deindustrialisation {#deindustrialisation}

**Deindustrialisation** means the decline of India's traditional handicraft industries in the 19th century, without the growth of modern industry to replace them. India had long been famous for cotton and silk textiles (for example the fine muslins of Dhaka and the silks of Murshidabad).

### Causes
- **Charter Act of 1813**: ended the Company's monopoly of trade with India (it kept its monopoly of the tea trade and the trade with China). British merchants and goods could now enter India freely.
- **One-way free trade**: cheap machine-made British goods entered India at low duties, while Indian goods entering Britain faced **high duties**.
- **Industrial Revolution** in Britain made factory cloth far cheaper than handloom cloth.
- **Loss of patronage**: the decline of Indian princely courts and nobility reduced demand for luxury handicrafts.
- **Railways** later carried British goods deep into the interior, reaching village markets.
- Pressure on weavers by Company agents (gomasthas) in the early period, who forced them to sell at low prices.

### Effects
- Ruin of weavers, spinners and other artisans; old manufacturing towns declined.
- Displaced artisans turned to agriculture, increasing **pressure on land** (often called the **ruralisation** of the economy).
- India became an exporter of **raw cotton** and an importer of finished cloth.

### Growth of modern industry
Modern factory industry developed slowly from the second half of the 19th century:
- First modern **cotton mill**: **Bombay, 1854** (Cowasjee Nanabhoy Davar; some textbooks give 1853).
- First **jute mill**: **Rishra (Bengal), 1855**.
Jute mills were mostly British-owned; cotton mills in Bombay and Ahmedabad were largely Indian-owned.

:::caution
Standard texts credit **Bombay** (C.N. Davar's mill), not Calcutta, with the first modern cotton mill. Bengal's early modern factory industry was **jute**.
:::

## Commercialisation of Agriculture {#commercialisation}

**Commercialisation of agriculture** means growing crops for sale in the market (often for export) rather than for the family's own use. Under British rule it was driven largely by the needs of British industry and trade, and by the need to pay land revenue in cash.

### Major commercial crops
- **Indigo**: grown in Bengal and Bihar for the dye needed by British textile mills. European planters forced peasants to grow it on unfair terms.
  - **Indigo Revolt (1859–60)** in Bengal: peasants refused to grow indigo. **Dinabandhu Mitra's** play **"Nil Darpan"** (1860) exposed the planters' oppression; **Harish Chandra Mukherjee's** paper **Hindoo Patriot** supported the ryots.
  - In **Champaran (Bihar)**, the **tinkathia** system forced peasants to grow indigo on **3/20th** of their land; Gandhi took up their cause in **1917**.
- **Cotton**: exported as raw material. During the **American Civil War (1861–65)**, supplies of American cotton to Britain were cut off and Indian cotton was in high demand. When the boom ended, many Deccan peasants fell into debt, contributing to the **Deccan Riots (1875)** against moneylenders.
- **Jute**: grown in Bengal; supplied jute mills in Britain (Dundee) and later in Bengal.
- **Opium**: the Company held a **monopoly** of opium production in Bengal and Bihar and exported it to **China**, which helped pay for British purchases of Chinese tea.
- **Tea** (Assam) and **coffee** were grown on European-owned plantations.

### Effects
- Benefits went mainly to traders, moneylenders and exporters rather than cultivators.
- Food crops were sometimes displaced, making some regions more vulnerable to **famine**.
- Peasants became dependent on world prices and fell into **debt**.

:::exam-tip
Link each crop with its event: indigo with **Nil Darpan** and **Champaran**, cotton with the **American Civil War** and the **Deccan Riots**, opium with the **China trade**.
:::

## Railways {#railways}

Railways were the largest field of British investment in India. They were planned mainly to serve British commercial, administrative and military interests rather than India's industrial development.

:::timeline
- 1853 | **Lord Dalhousie's** Railway Minute recommends a network of trunk lines
- 16 Apr 1853 | First passenger train in India: **Bombay to Thane** (about 34 km)
- 15 Aug 1854 | Railway line from **Howrah to Hooghly** opened in eastern India
:::

### Purpose
- To link the **interior** with the **ports**: raw materials (cotton, jute, grain) went out, and British manufactured goods came in.
- To move **troops** quickly and strengthen administrative control.
- To provide a profitable outlet for **British capital**.

### Guarantee system
Private British companies built the early railways. The government **guaranteed a minimum rate of interest** (generally 5%) on their capital, paid from Indian revenues whether or not the line made a profit. This guaranteed interest was counted by nationalists as part of the **drain of wealth**.

### Effects
- Speeded up the ruin of handicrafts by carrying cheap British goods to village markets (**deindustrialisation**).
- Encouraged **commercialisation of agriculture** by linking farms to export markets.
- Rates were fixed to favour traffic to and from the ports rather than internal trade.
- An unintended result: easier travel and contact helped Indians from different regions to meet, which aided the growth of **national consciousness**.
- Railways made it possible to move grain to famine-hit areas, though relief remained inadequate for a long time.

:::caution
The first passenger train ran from **Bombay to Thane** in **1853**, under Governor-General **Lord Dalhousie**. The Howrah–Hooghly line in Bengal opened in **1854**.
:::

## Famines {#famines}

Famines were frequent under British rule. Crop failure due to drought was the immediate trigger, but standard texts point to deeper causes: heavy land revenue, poverty and indebtedness of peasants, decline of handicrafts, the shift to commercial crops and the government's weak relief policy for much of the 19th century.

### Major famines (selected)
- **Great Bengal Famine of 1770**: known in Bengali as the "Famine of '76" (Bengali year 1176). It occurred under the **dual system of government** after the Diwani; contemporary estimates put deaths at about one-third of the population of Bengal. Revenue collection continued harshly.
- **Great Famine of 1876–78**: affected Madras, Bombay, Mysore and Hyderabad, among other areas; it occurred during the viceroyalty of **Lord Lytton**.
- **Famine of 1899–1900**: widespread, during **Lord Curzon's** time.
- **Bengal Famine of 1943** (known in Bengali as *Panchasher Manwantar*): during the Second World War.

### Famine Commissions

| Year | Chairman | Context |
|---|---|---|
| 1880 (report; appointed 1878) | **Sir Richard Strachey** | After the famine of 1876–78; recommendations led to **Famine Codes** (the first in 1883) |
| 1898 | **Sir James Lyall** | After the famine of 1896–97 |
| 1901 | **Sir Antony MacDonnell** | After the famine of 1899–1900 (Lord Curzon) |
| 1944 | **Sir John Woodhead** | Inquiry into the Bengal Famine of 1943 |

:::keyfacts
- First Famine Commission: **1880**, **Richard Strachey**, under **Lord Lytton**
- Famine Codes: first framed in **1883**
- **Chhiyattorer Manwantar** ("Famine of '76") = the Bengal famine of **1770**
:::

:::exam-tip
Arrange the Famine Commissions in order with the initials **S–L–M–W** (Strachey 1880, Lyall 1898, MacDonnell 1901, Woodhead 1944).
:::
