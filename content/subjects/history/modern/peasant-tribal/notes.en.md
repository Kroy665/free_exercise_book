# Peasant and Tribal Movements (to 1900)

## Causes and Nature {#causes}

Company rule changed life in the countryside far more deeply than earlier rulers had. From the late 18th century onward, peasants and tribal communities rose again and again against the new order. These risings came long before organised nationalism and carried on alongside it.

### Why peasants revolted
- **High land revenue** under the Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari and Mahalwari systems, collected rigidly even in years of bad harvest or famine.
- The rise of **zamindars, moneylenders and traders** who gained from the new land laws. Peasants who fell into debt often lost their land to them.
- **Commercial crops forced on cultivators**, as in the case of indigo in Bengal.
- **Illegal cesses**, rent increases and evictions by landlords.

### Why tribal communities revolted
- Tribal areas had their own systems of **collective land ownership** and self-rule. British laws treated land as private property that could be sold or mortgaged.
- **Outsiders** (called *dikus* by the tribes of Chotanagpur and the Santhal country), such as moneylenders, traders, contractors and landlords, entered tribal lands with the backing of British courts and police.
- **Forest rules** restricted shifting cultivation, hunting and the collection of forest produce.
- Christian missionary activity was seen by some tribal leaders as a threat to their customs.

### General nature
- Most movements were aimed at the **immediate oppressor**, the zamindar, moneylender or planter, rather than at colonial rule as a whole. When the State protected these groups, the rebels clashed with it too.
- They were **local and scattered**, and there was little coordination between regions.
- Leaders often drew on **religion, prophecy or messianic hopes** to unite their followers.
- Tribal revolts were usually **more violent** and were put down with great force.
- From the 1850s, the **educated middle class and the press** began to support some peasant causes (Indigo Revolt, Pabna, Deccan), and the peasants themselves turned more to **legal methods**.

:::exam-tip
Historians such as Bipan Chandra point out that 19th-century peasants generally fought the **local agents** of exploitation and did not yet see **colonialism** as the root cause. Statement-type questions often test this point.
:::

## Sanyasi-Fakir, Chuar and Paika Revolts {#early-revolts}

The earliest major resistance came soon after the Company took control of Bengal's revenue in 1765.

### Sanyasi-Fakir rebellion (Bengal, c. 1763–1800)
- Bands of **Hindu sanyasis and Muslim fakirs**, many of whom had land grants and moved between pilgrimage centres, clashed with the Company in north Bengal and Bihar.
- Causes: the devastating **Bengal famine of 1770**, the Company's harsh revenue demands, and **restrictions on visits to holy places**. Many displaced peasants and disbanded soldiers joined them.
- Leaders included **Majnu Shah** (a fakir), **Bhawani Pathak** and **Debi Chaudhurani**.
- The movement wore down only towards the end of the 18th century after repeated military campaigns.
- **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay's** novel ***Anandamath*** (1882), which contains the song *Vande Mataram*, is set against this rebellion.

### Chuar uprisings (Jungle Mahal)
- The Chuars were tribal and semi-tribal people of the **Jungle Mahal** region (parts of Midnapore, Bankura and Manbhum).
- They rose against higher revenue demands and the loss of land granted to them for police and military service. There were risings in the **1760s–70s** and a larger one in the **late 1790s**.
- Leaders included **Durjan Singh**; Bengali textbooks also highlight **Rani Shiromani** of Karnagarh.

### Paika rebellion (Odisha, 1817)
- The **Paikas** were the traditional landed militia of Odisha, who held rent-free land in return for military service to the Raja of **Khurda**.
- After the British conquest of Odisha (1803), they lost these lands, revenue was raised and the price of salt rose.
- In **1817**, led by **Bakshi Jagabandhu** (Jagabandhu Bidyadhar), the Paikas rose in revolt. The rising was suppressed, but it is remembered in Odisha as an early armed challenge to the Company.

:::caution
The Sanyasi-Fakir rebellion was not a religious war between communities. Hindu sanyasis and Muslim fakirs fought **together** against the Company.
:::

## Kol Uprising and Santhal Hul {#kol-santhal}

Both risings took place in the forest and hill belt of present-day **Jharkhand** and its borderlands, where tribal land was passing to outsiders.

### Kol uprising (1831–32)
- Area: **Chotanagpur** (Ranchi, Singhbhum, Hazaribagh, Palamau and Manbhum).
- Cause: tribal land was transferred to **outsiders** (farmers, merchants and moneylenders brought in under British protection), and tribal chiefs lost control over their villages.
- Leaders included **Buddhu Bhagat**.
- The rising was crushed by the army. Afterwards, the British placed the region under a separate, simpler **non-regulation** administration (the South-West Frontier Agency) to manage it more directly.

### Santhal Hul (1855–56)
- **Area**: the **Damin-i-koh** tract around the **Rajmahal hills** (in the Bhagalpur–Birbhum region), where the Santhals had cleared forest and settled.
- **Causes**: zamindars, moneylenders and traders (the *dikus*) cheated the Santhals with high interest and false weights; police and courts sided with the outsiders; Santhals were forced into debt bondage and lost their land.
- **Leaders**: the brothers **Sidhu and Kanhu** (Sido and Kanhu Murmu), along with their brothers **Chand and Bhairav**.
- On **30 June 1855**, thousands of Santhals gathered at **Bhognadih** and declared a **Hul** (rebellion). They vowed to drive out the outsiders and set up their own rule.
- The British declared **martial law** and crushed the rebellion by **1856**; thousands of Santhals were killed. Both Sidhu and Kanhu lost their lives in the suppression.
- **Result**: a separate district, the **Santhal Parganas**, was created, with special rules to protect Santhal land.

:::keyfacts
- Kol uprising: **1831–32**, Chotanagpur; leader **Buddhu Bhagat**
- Santhal Hul: **1855–56**, Rajmahal hills (Damin-i-koh); leaders **Sidhu and Kanhu**
- 30 June is observed as **Hul Diwas**
- Outcome of the Hul: the **Santhal Parganas** district
:::

:::caution
Do not mix up the Santhal Hul (**1855–56**) with the Munda Ulgulan (**1899–1900**). The words *Hul* and *Ulgulan* both mean rebellion or great tumult, but belong to different tribes and periods.
:::

## Munda Ulgulan and Birsa Munda {#munda}

The Munda rising was the last great tribal rebellion of the 19th century in Chotanagpur.

### Background
- The Mundas traditionally held land jointly under the **khuntkatti** system, in which the village lineage that had cleared the forest owned the land in common.
- Over the 19th century, **jagirdars, thikadars (revenue farmers), moneylenders and traders** from outside took over Munda land and turned the cultivators into tenants. They also demanded **forced labour** (*beth begari*).
- Christian missions offered some help, but many Mundas felt that neither the missionaries nor the courts restored their lost rights.

### Birsa Munda (1875–1900)
- Born in the Ranchi region in **1875**, Birsa came into contact with both Christian missionaries and Vaishnava teachers.
- From the mid-1890s he preached a reformed faith: give up liquor and superstition, follow a pure life, and believe in one God. His followers saw him as a prophet and called him ***Dharti Aba*** ("father of the earth").
- He called on the Mundas to stop paying rent and to drive out the *dikus*, the missionaries and the British, and to set up a **Munda Raj**.

### The Ulgulan (1899–1900)
- At the end of **1899**, the Mundas attacked churches, police stations and the property of landlords and moneylenders around Ranchi.
- The British crushed the rising early in **1900**. Birsa was arrested and **died in Ranchi jail in June 1900**.

### Aftermath
- The government recorded Munda land rights and passed the **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act, 1908**. It recognised **khuntkatti** rights and banned *beth begari*.
- Birsa Munda's birth anniversary, **15 November**, is observed as **Janjatiya Gaurav Divas** (since 2021); Jharkhand state was also formed on 15 November 2000.

:::keyfacts
- **Ulgulan** = "great tumult"; **1899–1900**; region: around **Ranchi**
- Leader: **Birsa Munda**, called **Dharti Aba**
- Land system defended: **khuntkatti** (joint ownership)
- Outcome: **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act, 1908**
:::

## Wahabi and Faraizi Movements {#wahabi-faraizi}

These were religious reform movements among Indian Muslims that, in parts of Bengal and north India, also turned into resistance against landlords, planters and British authority. Historians differ on how far they were religious, agrarian or political; most textbooks describe all three elements.

### Wahabi movement
- Founder in India: **Syed Ahmad of Rae Bareli** (1786–1831). He was influenced by the teachings of **Shah Waliullah** of Delhi. Its followers called it *Tariqah-i-Muhammadiya* ("the path of the Prophet Muhammad"); the name *Wahabi*, used by the British and by most textbooks, links it to the Arabian reformer Abdul Wahab.
- It called for a return to the original teachings of Islam and opposed what it saw as later additions.
- **Patna** became its main centre. Syed Ahmad first fought the Sikh kingdom in the north-west and was killed at **Balakot (1831)**.
- After the annexation of Punjab (1849), the movement turned against the British. It was put down through a series of military operations and **conspiracy trials in the 1860s and early 1870s**.

### Titu Mir and the Barasat uprising (1831)
- **Titu Mir** (Mir Nisar Ali), a follower of Syed Ahmad, organised the peasants of the **24 Parganas and Nadia** area against local **zamindars and indigo planters**, who imposed illegal taxes on them.
- He built a **bamboo fort at Narkelberia** near Barasat. In **1831** British troops destroyed the fort and Titu Mir was killed.

### Faraizi movement (eastern Bengal)
- Founded by **Haji Shariatullah** of **Faridpur** early in the 19th century. The name comes from *faraiz*, the **obligatory religious duties** of Islam, which the movement urged followers to observe.
- Under his son **Dudu Miyan** (Muhsin-ud-din Ahmad), it became an **agrarian movement**. He declared that **land belongs to God** and that no one had the right to levy taxes on it, and he urged peasants not to pay illegal cesses to zamindars.
- Dudu Miyan built a network of agents to settle disputes and organise peasants. He was jailed several times; he died in **1862**, after which the movement became mainly religious again.

:::caution
**Titu Mir** was linked to the **Wahabi** (Syed Ahmad) tradition and was active near **Barasat**. **Haji Shariatullah** and **Dudu Miyan** led the **Faraizi** movement based in **Faridpur**. Questions often swap these pairs.
:::

## The Indigo Revolt, 1859–60 {#indigo}

The Indigo Revolt (*Nil Bidroha*) in Bengal is often described as the most widespread and successful peasant movement of 19th-century Bengal.

### Causes
- European **planters** forced ryots to grow indigo on their best land, paying cash **advances** (*dadan*) that trapped them in debt.
- The price paid for indigo was far below its value, and refusal brought **kidnapping, beatings, burning of houses and destruction of crops** by the planters' armed retainers.
- The courts and many officials tended to side with the planters.

### Course of the revolt
- In **1859**, peasants in **Nadia** district refused to sow indigo. Two leaders of **Govindpur** village, **Digambar Biswas and Bishnucharan Biswas**, are widely remembered as early organisers.
- The movement spread to **Jessore, Pabna, Murshidabad** and other districts. Peasants resisted the planters' men, refused advances, and went to court collectively.
- Hindu and Muslim ryots took part together; some small zamindars also helped.

### Support from the educated class
- **Dinabandhu Mitra's** play ***Nil Darpan*** (1860) portrayed the planters' cruelty and moved public opinion.
- Rev. **James Long** published an English translation; he was tried for libel, **fined and imprisoned**.
- **Harish Chandra Mukherjee**, editor of the ***Hindoo Patriot***, campaigned for the ryots.

### Result
- The government set up the **Indigo Commission (1860)**, and declared that ryots **could not be compelled** to grow indigo.
- By the end of the 1860s, indigo cultivation in Bengal had declined sharply; planters shifted to **Bihar**, where the issue returned in **Champaran (1917)**.

:::keyfacts
- Indigo Revolt: **1859–60**, Bengal (began in **Nadia**)
- Local leaders: **Digambar and Bishnucharan Biswas** (Govindpur)
- Play: ***Nil Darpan*** by **Dinabandhu Mitra**
- Press support: **Harish Chandra Mukherjee** (*Hindoo Patriot*)
- Government response: **Indigo Commission, 1860**
:::

:::exam-tip
Remember that *Nil Darpan* is a **play** by Dinabandhu Mitra, not a newspaper. *Hindoo Patriot* is the newspaper, edited by Harish Chandra Mukherjee.
:::

## Pabna Revolt and Deccan Riots {#pabna-deccan}

By the 1870s, peasant protest increasingly used **legal methods** and won support from the educated middle class.

### Pabna agrarian leagues (1873–76)
- Area: the **Yusufshahi pargana** of **Pabna** district in eastern Bengal.
- Cause: zamindars **raised rents illegally** and tried to stop tenants from gaining **occupancy rights**, which the Rent Act of 1859 (Act X of 1859) gave to ryots who had cultivated the same land for twelve years.
- In **1873**, the peasants formed an **agrarian league** (union). It organised **rent strikes**, raised funds and fought the zamindars in the **courts**.
- Leaders included **Ishan Chandra Roy, Shambhu Pal and Khoodi Mollah**. Both Hindu and Muslim peasants took part.
- The struggle was largely **peaceful and legal**. The peasants did not oppose the British; they said they wished to be ryots of the Queen alone.
- Supporters among the intelligentsia included the **Indian Association** (founded in Calcutta in **1876** by Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose) and writers such as **R.C. Dutt** and **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay**.
- Result: it prepared the ground for the **Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885**.

### Deccan riots (1875)
- Area: **Poona and Ahmednagar** districts of the Bombay Deccan, under the **Ryotwari** system.
- Causes: a **revenue increase** in the late 1860s; the end of the **cotton boom** after the **American Civil War** (1861–65) brought prices down; peasants fell deep into debt to **moneylenders** (mostly Marwari and Gujarati *sahukars*), who used the courts to take their land.
- Peasants first organised a **social boycott** of the moneylenders. In **May 1875**, at **Supa** village (Poona district), this turned into riots. Peasants attacked moneylenders' houses and **seized and burnt their bonds and debt deeds**.
- There was little killing; the target was the **documents** of debt.
- Moderate nationalists of the **Poona Sarvajanik Sabha** supported the ryots.
- Result: the **Deccan Riots Commission** and the **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879**.

:::caution
The Deccan riots were aimed at **moneylenders**, not zamindars; the Bombay Deccan was under the Ryotwari system. The Pabna movement was aimed at **zamindars** in Permanently Settled Bengal.
:::

## How the British Responded {#british-response}

The government's response combined **force** with **limited reform**. As a rule, armed tribal risings were crushed first, while later peasant movements that used peaceful methods led to inquiries and new laws.

### Repression
- The army and police were used against the Sanyasi-Fakirs, Chuars, Paikas, Kols, Santhals and Mundas. **Martial law** was declared during the Santhal Hul.
- Leaders such as Titu Mir, Sidhu and Kanhu were killed, and Birsa Munda died in prison.
- The Wahabis were pursued through military campaigns and conspiracy trials.

### Special administration for tribal areas
- After the Kol uprising, Chotanagpur was placed under a separate non-regulation administration.
- After the Hul, the **Santhal Parganas** were formed as a separate district.
- These arrangements aimed to govern tribal areas more directly and to limit the entry of outsiders.

### Laws and commissions
| Movement | British response |
|---|---|
| Indigo Revolt (1859–60) | **Indigo Commission, 1860**; ryots could not be forced to grow indigo |
| Pabna (1873) | Ground prepared for the **Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885** |
| Deccan riots (1875) | **Deccan Riots Commission**; **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879** |
| Munda Ulgulan (1899–1900) | **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act, 1908** |

- The **Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885** strengthened the occupancy rights of tenants and limited arbitrary rent increases by zamindars.
- The **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879** gave peasants some protection against moneylenders, for example by limiting the seizure of their land for debt.

### Limits
These laws did not change the basic land revenue system. Peasants remained poor and indebted, which is why agrarian protest returned in new forms in the 20th century, now linked with the national movement.
