# Expansion of British Power

## Bengal and the Battle of Plassey {#plassey}

Bengal was the richest province of the Mughal Empire and the most profitable base of the English East India Company. Under Murshid Quli Khan and later **Alivardi Khan**, the Nawabs of Bengal had become practically independent of Delhi. When Alivardi died in **1756**, his grandson **Siraj-ud-Daulah** became Nawab and soon clashed with the Company.

### Causes of conflict
- The Company **fortified Calcutta** (Fort William) without the Nawab's permission.
- Company servants **misused the *dastak*** (duty-free trade pass granted by the Mughal *farman* of 1717) for their private trade, causing the Nawab a loss of revenue.
- The English gave **shelter to people the Nawab wanted**, such as Krishna Das, son of a disgraced official.
- The Company backed rivals of Siraj within the Nawab's court.

### Fall of Calcutta and the "Black Hole"
In **June 1756** Siraj captured Calcutta. According to **J.Z. Holwell**, an English survivor, many English prisoners were locked in a small, airless room overnight and most of them died. This is called the **Black Hole incident**. Historians doubt Holwell's figures, and the scale of the event is **disputed**; it was later used heavily in British propaganda.

### Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757)
- **Robert Clive** and Admiral Watson retook Calcutta in early 1757; the Nawab agreed to the **Treaty of Alinagar** (February 1757).
- The English then captured the French settlement of **Chandernagore** (March 1757).
- Clive conspired with **Mir Jafar** (the Nawab's commander), **Rai Durlabh**, the banker **Jagat Seth** and the merchant **Omichand (Amir Chand)**.
- At **Plassey (Palashi)** on the Bhagirathi, a large part of the Nawab's army under Mir Jafar did not fight. Siraj fled, was captured and put to death.

:::keyfacts
- Battle of Plassey: **23 June 1757**; English commander: **Robert Clive**
- Nawab defeated: **Siraj-ud-Daulah**; traitor-commander: **Mir Jafar**
- Plassey was won more by **conspiracy** than by fighting
:::

:::caution
The Black Hole story rests mainly on Holwell's account. Do not treat his numbers as established fact.
:::

## Mir Jafar and Mir Qasim {#mir-qasim}

Plassey made the Company the real power behind the throne of Bengal. The Nawabs who followed depended on English support, and each change of Nawab brought the Company more money and land.

### Mir Jafar (1757–1760)
- Became Nawab with English backing. He gave the Company the **zamindari of the 24 Parganas** and paid large sums to the Company and its officials; Clive received a personal *jagir*.
- He could not meet the Company's growing money demands and was replaced in **1760** by his son-in-law, **Mir Qasim**.

### Mir Qasim (1760–1763)
- On becoming Nawab, he ceded the districts of **Burdwan, Midnapore and Chittagong** to the Company.
- He was an able ruler who tried to be independent: he **shifted his capital from Murshidabad to Monghyr (Munger)**, reorganised the finances and built a modern, disciplined army.
- Company servants kept misusing the *dastak* for their private inland trade, which hurt Indian merchants and the Nawab's income.
- Mir Qasim responded by **abolishing all internal duties** on trade, so Indian merchants were placed on an equal footing with the English. The English treated this as a challenge to their privileges.
- War broke out in **1763**. Mir Qasim was defeated in a series of battles, and the English **restored Mir Jafar** as Nawab. Mir Qasim fled to **Awadh** and sought allies there.

:::exam-tip
Remember the cause of Mir Qasim's quarrel: he did not grant extra privileges, he **removed duties for everyone**, which ended the English advantage. This is a favourite assertion–reason theme.
:::

## Battle of Buxar and the Treaty of Allahabad {#buxar}

Mir Qasim formed an alliance with **Shuja-ud-Daulah**, the Nawab of **Awadh**, and the Mughal emperor **Shah Alam II**. Their combined army met the English forces under **Major Hector Munro** at **Buxar** (in present-day Bihar) in **October 1764** and was defeated.

### Why Buxar mattered
- At Plassey the English won mainly through conspiracy. At Buxar they defeated the **combined forces of three Indian powers**, including the Mughal emperor, in an open battle.
- Many historians therefore regard Buxar as **more decisive** than Plassey: it confirmed English supremacy in Bengal and opened the way into north India.

### Treaties of Allahabad (August 1765)
**Robert Clive**, back in India as Governor of Bengal, signed two treaties at Allahabad:

| With | Main terms |
|---|---|
| **Shah Alam II** (Mughal emperor) | Granted the **Diwani** (right to collect revenue) of **Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** to the Company; in return the Company would pay him **₹26 lakh a year** and give him the districts of **Kora and Allahabad** |
| **Shuja-ud-Daulah** (Nawab of Awadh) | Gave up Allahabad and Kora (for the emperor); paid **₹50 lakh** as war indemnity; recognised **Balwant Singh** of Banaras in his estate; Awadh itself was **returned** to Shuja |

Awadh was kept as a **buffer state** between Bengal and the Marathas and Afghans rather than annexed.

:::keyfacts
- Battle of Buxar: **1764**; English commander: **Hector Munro**
- Defeated allies: **Mir Qasim + Shuja-ud-Daulah + Shah Alam II**
- Treaty of Allahabad: **1765**, signed by **Clive**; Company obtained the **Diwani**
:::

:::caution
The Diwani was granted by the **Mughal emperor Shah Alam II**, not by the Nawab of Bengal or the Nawab of Awadh.
:::

## Diwani and the Dual Government {#dual-government}

After 1765 the Company held the **Diwani** (revenue administration) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. The **Nizamat** (police, criminal justice and general administration) nominally stayed with the Nawab of Bengal, who by then was a pensioner of the Company. This arrangement, set up by **Clive**, is called the **Dual (Double) Government** of Bengal (1765–1772).

### How it worked
- The Company appointed Indian deputies: **Muhammad Reza Khan** in Bengal and **Raja Shitab Rai** in Bihar.
- The Company had **power without responsibility**: it collected the revenue but did not answer for administration.
- The Nawab had **responsibility without power**: he was blamed for disorder but had neither the money nor the army to govern.

### Results
- Revenue collection became harsh as officials tried to raise as much as possible.
- Trade, crafts and agriculture declined, and the administration broke down.
- The **Bengal famine of 1770** (known in Bengal as the *Chhiattorer Manwantar*) killed a very large part of the population, while revenue demands stayed high.

### End of the system
**Warren Hastings** ended the Dual Government in **1772**. The Company took over the administration directly, and the treasury was moved from **Murshidabad to Calcutta**.

:::keyfacts
- Dual Government: introduced by **Clive** (1765), abolished by **Warren Hastings** (1772)
- Diwani = revenue; Nizamat = police and justice
:::

## Anglo-Mysore Wars {#mysore}

In the south, Mysore rose under **Hyder Ali**, a soldier who took real control of the Wodeyar kingdom around **1761**, and his son **Tipu Sultan**. Both built strong, modern armies with French help and were serious rivals to the Company in south India.

| War | Years | British side | Main events | Treaty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | 1767–69 | Madras government | Hyder Ali reached close to Madras | **Treaty of Madras (1769)**: mutual restoration of conquests and a defensive alliance |
| Second | 1780–84 | Warren Hastings (Governor-General) | Hyder defeated Baillie (1780); Eyre Coote defeated Hyder at Porto Novo (1781); **Hyder died in 1782**; Tipu continued the war | **Treaty of Mangalore (1784)**: mutual restoration of conquests |
| Third | 1790–92 | **Lord Cornwallis**, allied with the Nizam and the Marathas | Siege of Seringapatam | **Treaty of Seringapatam (1792)**: Tipu ceded about **half his territory**, paid a war indemnity and gave two sons as hostages |
| Fourth | 1799 | **Lord Wellesley** | Seringapatam stormed; **Tipu killed** defending his capital (May 1799) | Wodeyar dynasty restored; Mysore brought under the **Subsidiary Alliance** |

### Tipu Sultan
- Ruled from **Seringapatam (Srirangapatna)**.
- Sought help from the French and planted a **"tree of liberty"** at Seringapatam, showing his interest in the French Revolution.
- In 1785 Tipu banned exports of sandalwood, pepper and cardamom from Mysore's ports and barred his merchants from dealing with the Company, hurting its trade.

:::mnemonic
Mysore treaties in order: **M**adras, **M**angalore, **S**eringapatam: "**M**ysore **M**en **S**tood" (1769, 1784, 1792), then Tipu fell in 1799.
:::

:::caution
Hyder Ali died during the **Second** war (1782). Tipu died in the **Fourth** war (1799), not the Third.
:::

## Anglo-Maratha Wars {#maratha}

After their defeat at the **Third Battle of Panipat (1761)** the Marathas recovered, but their confederacy (the Peshwa at Pune with the Scindia, Holkar, Bhonsle and Gaekwad) was divided by succession disputes. The Company used these quarrels to intervene.

### First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782)
- Began when the Bombay government supported **Raghunath Rao (Raghoba)**, a claimant to the Peshwaship (**Treaty of Surat, 1775**).
- The Calcutta council, under Warren Hastings, signed the **Treaty of Purandar (1776)**, but fighting resumed.
- Ended by the **Treaty of Salbai (1782)**, mediated by **Mahadji Scindia**: largely a restoration of the **status quo**. Raghunath Rao was given a pension, and the Company kept **Salsette**.
- Result: about **twenty years of peace** between the two powers.

### Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805)
- After Holkar defeated him, Peshwa **Baji Rao II** fled to the English and signed the **Treaty of Bassein (31 December 1802)**, accepting the **Subsidiary Alliance**.
- The Scindia and the Bhonsle saw this as a surrender of Maratha independence and fought the English.
- Arthur Wellesley won at **Assaye (1803)**; Lord Lake won at **Laswari (1803)**. The Bhonsle and the Scindia signed separate treaties and accepted British terms.

### Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818)
- Fought under **Lord Hastings** (Marquess of Hastings) during his campaign against the Pindaris.
- The Peshwa was defeated; the **office of Peshwa was abolished** and Baji Rao II was pensioned off to **Bithur** near Kanpur.
- Maratha power came to an end and the Company became the paramount power in India.

:::keyfacts
- Treaty of Salbai: **1782**, ended the First war
- Treaty of Bassein: **1802**, Baji Rao II accepted the Subsidiary Alliance
- Third war: **1817–18**, Peshwaship abolished
:::

## Subsidiary Alliance {#subsidiary-alliance}

The **Subsidiary Alliance** was the main tool used by **Lord Wellesley** (Governor-General 1798–1805) to bring Indian states under British control without fighting a war against each of them. (The idea of lending troops to Indian rulers had earlier been used by the French governor **Dupleix**.)

### Terms for the Indian ally
- A **British force was stationed** in the state; the ruler paid for it, in cash or by **ceding territory**.
- A **British Resident** was posted at the ruler's court.
- The ruler could **not employ other Europeans** (especially the French) without British approval.
- The ruler could **not make war, peace or alliances** with other powers without British consent; the Company handled his foreign relations.
- In return, the Company promised to **protect** the state from external attack and (in principle) not to interfere in internal affairs.

### States that accepted it
| State | Year |
|---|---|
| **Hyderabad** (Nizam) | **1798**, the first |
| Mysore | 1799 (after Tipu's defeat) |
| Awadh | 1801 (ceded Rohilkhand and the lower Doab) |
| Peshwa | 1802 (Treaty of Bassein) |

### Effects
- The Indian states lost their **independence** in foreign affairs and their armies were disbanded, leaving many soldiers unemployed.
- Payments for the troops drained the states' finances; failure to pay often led to **loss of territory**.
- The Company gained a large army paid for by Indian rulers and pushed its frontiers forward.

:::exam-tip
The first state to accept the Subsidiary Alliance was **Hyderabad (1798)**. Mysore accepted it only after the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War.
:::

## Anglo-Sikh Wars and the annexation of Punjab {#punjab}

**Maharaja Ranjit Singh** united the Sikh *misls* and built a powerful kingdom in Punjab with its capital at **Lahore**. By the **Treaty of Amritsar (1809)** with the Company, the **Sutlej** was accepted as the boundary, and he did not expand to its south. After his death in **1839**, Punjab fell into political instability.

### First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–1846)
- Governor-General: **Lord Hardinge**.
- Battles: Mudki, Ferozeshah, Aliwal and Sobraon.
- **Treaty of Lahore (1846)**: the Sikhs ceded the **Jullundur Doab**, paid a war indemnity and had to reduce their army. The minor **Dalip Singh** remained Maharaja; from the end of 1846 (Treaty of Bhairowal) a British Resident controlled the government at Lahore. Kashmir was transferred to **Gulab Singh** of Jammu.

### Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–1849)
- Governor-General: **Lord Dalhousie**.
- Began with a revolt at **Multan** under its governor **Mulraj**.
- Battles: **Chillianwala** (1849, heavy losses on both sides) and **Gujarat** (1849, decisive British victory).
- **Punjab was annexed in 1849**. Dalip Singh was pensioned off.

:::keyfacts
- Treaty of Amritsar (with Ranjit Singh): **1809**
- First war ended by the **Treaty of Lahore (1846)**
- Punjab annexed: **1849**, under **Dalhousie**, after the Second war
:::

:::caution
Punjab was **not** annexed after the First war. It was annexed in 1849, after the Second war.
:::

## Dalhousie and the Doctrine of Lapse {#doctrine-of-lapse}

**Lord Dalhousie** (Governor-General 1848–1856) expanded British territory faster than any of his predecessors, by war, by the **Doctrine of Lapse** and on grounds of misgovernance.

### Doctrine of Lapse
- If the ruler of a **dependent state** died **without a natural male heir**, the state would **"lapse"** to the Company. An **adopted son** could inherit the ruler's private property but **not the kingdom**.
- Adoption was traditional and widely accepted in Indian ruling families, so the policy caused deep resentment.

| State | Year annexed |
|---|---|
| **Satara** | **1848**, the first |
| Sambalpur | 1849 |
| **Jhansi** | 1853 |
| **Nagpur** | 1854 |

### Other annexations under Dalhousie
- **Punjab** (1849), after the Second Anglo-Sikh War.
- **Lower Burma** (1852), after the Second Anglo-Burmese War.
- **Berar** (1853), taken from the Nizam of Hyderabad in lieu of unpaid dues for the subsidiary force.
- **Awadh** (1856): Nawab **Wajid Ali Shah** was removed on the charge of **misgovernance**. This was **not** an application of the Doctrine of Lapse. Its consequences are covered in the topic *The Revolt of 1857*.
- Dalhousie also stopped the pensions and titles of several former rulers, including the pension of **Nana Saheb**, adopted son of Baji Rao II.

:::caution
Awadh had a ruling Nawab and heirs, so the Doctrine of Lapse could not apply. It was annexed in **1856** on grounds of **misgovernance**.
:::
