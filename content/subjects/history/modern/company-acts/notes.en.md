# Company Rule: Acts of 1773–1853

## Background: why Parliament stepped in {#background}

The East India Company began as a trading body, but after the battles of **Plassey (1757)** and **Buxar (1764)** and the grant of the **Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa (1765)** it became a territorial power. A private company was now collecting revenue and ruling millions of people, yet the British Parliament had little say over how it did so.

Several problems pushed Parliament to act:
- **Corruption and private trade** by Company servants, many of whom returned to Britain very rich.
- The **Bengal famine of 1770**, which caused huge loss of life and reduced revenue.
- The Company's **financial crisis**: it sought financial help from the British Government in the early 1770s.
- A growing view in Britain that a trading company should not exercise sovereign powers without control.

From 1773 onwards, Parliament passed a series of Acts. Each step increased the **control of the British Government** over the Company, **centralised** the administration in India and **reduced the Company's commercial role**, until the Crown took over directly in 1858.

:::timeline
- 1773 | Regulating Act
- 1784 | Pitt's India Act
- 1786 | Act of 1786 (powers for Lord Cornwallis)
- 1793 | Charter Act of 1793
- 1813 | Charter Act of 1813
- 1833 | Charter Act of 1833
- 1853 | Charter Act of 1853 (the last Charter Act)
- 1858 | Government of India Act: Crown rule begins
:::

:::exam-tip
Remember the overall direction: **more Parliamentary control, more centralisation, less trade**. Most questions test which Act introduced which change.
:::

## Regulating Act, 1773 {#regulating-act}

The Regulating Act was the **first step by the British Parliament to control and regulate** the affairs of the Company in India. It recognised, for the first time, that the Company had political and administrative functions, and it laid the foundations of a central administration. It was passed when **Lord North** was the British Prime Minister.

### Main provisions
- The Governor of Bengal was made the **Governor-General of Bengal**, assisted by an **executive council of four members**. **Warren Hastings** became the first Governor-General of Bengal.
- The Governors of the **Bombay and Madras Presidencies** were made **subordinate** to the Governor-General of Bengal.
- A **Supreme Court** was set up at **Calcutta (Fort William) in 1774**, with a **Chief Justice and three other judges**. **Sir Elijah Impey** was its first Chief Justice.
- Company servants were **forbidden to engage in private trade** and to accept presents or bribes.
- The **Court of Directors** (the Company's governing body in London) had to report on the revenue, civil and military affairs in India to the British Government.

### Weaknesses
- The Governor-General had **no power to override** his council; he was often outvoted by the majority of his councillors.
- The relationship between the Supreme Court and the Governor-General-in-Council was not clearly defined, which led to conflicts.
- Control over Bombay and Madras remained weak in practice.

:::keyfacts
- First Governor-General of Bengal: **Warren Hastings**
- Supreme Court at Calcutta: **1774**, first Chief Justice **Sir Elijah Impey**
- Council of **four** members
:::

:::caution
The Regulating Act created the post of **Governor-General of Bengal**, **not** Governor-General of India. The latter came only with the Charter Act of **1833**.
:::

## Pitt's India Act, 1784 {#pitts-india-act}

The weaknesses of the Regulating Act led to a new law, named after the British Prime Minister **William Pitt the Younger**. Its main aim was to bring the Company's political affairs firmly under the British Government while leaving its trade with the Company.

### Main provisions
- It **separated the commercial and political functions** of the Company.
- The **Court of Directors** continued to manage **commercial affairs** and to make appointments.
- A new body, the **Board of Control**, was created to supervise and direct the Company's **civil and military government and revenues** in India. It had six members, including the Chancellor of the Exchequer and a Secretary of State.
- This arrangement is called the **dual system of government** (or double government): control was shared by the Board of Control (representing the British Government) and the Court of Directors (representing the Company).
- The Company's territories in India were called the **"British possessions in India"** for the first time.
- The Governor-General's council was **reduced to three members**.
- The Bombay and Madras Presidencies were made more clearly subordinate to the Governor-General in matters of war, diplomacy and revenue.

### Significance
The British Government now had supreme control over the Company's affairs and its administration in India. The dual system lasted until **1858**, when the Government of India Act abolished both the Board of Control and the Court of Directors.

:::caution
Do not confuse the **dual system of Pitt's India Act (1784)** (Board of Control + Court of Directors) with **Robert Clive's dual government in Bengal (1765–72)**, under which the Company held the Diwani while the Nawab remained responsible for administration.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Board of Control" = **Pitt's India Act, 1784**. It is a very common one-liner in SSC and state PSC papers.
:::

## Act of 1786 and Charter Act of 1793 {#acts-1786-1793}

### Act of 1786
**Lord Cornwallis** agreed to become Governor-General only if he was given stronger powers than Warren Hastings had. The Act of 1786 met his demands:
- The Governor-General could **override his council** in special cases, on his own responsibility.
- He could also hold the post of **Commander-in-Chief**.

This removed the main weakness of the Regulating Act, where the Governor-General could be outvoted by his own council.

### Charter Act of 1793
The Company's charter had to be renewed from time to time by Parliament. The Charter Act of 1793 did the following:
- **Renewed the Company's charter**, including its **trade monopoly** in the East, for another **20 years**.
- **Extended the overriding power** over the council to the Governor-General and also to the **Governors of the subordinate presidencies**.
- Gave the Governor-General **greater control over the Bombay and Madras** governments.
- Provided that the salaries of the members and staff of the **Board of Control** would be **paid out of Indian revenues**.

:::keyfacts
- Act of 1786: Governor-General can **override the council**; may also be **Commander-in-Chief** (for **Lord Cornwallis**)
- Charter Act of 1793: charter renewed for **20 years**; Board of Control salaries charged to **Indian revenues**
:::

:::caution
The Charter Act of 1793 **continued** the Company's trade monopoly. The monopoly was ended (with exceptions) only by the Charter Act of **1813**.
:::

:::mnemonic
Charters came every **20 years**: **1793 → 1813 → 1833 → 1853**. The 1853 Act broke the pattern by **not** fixing any period.
:::

## Charter Act of 1813 {#charter-act-1813}

By the early 19th century, British merchants and manufacturers wanted a share in Indian trade. Free-trade ideas were spreading in Britain, and **Napoleon's Continental System**, which closed European ports to British goods, made new markets more important. The Charter Act of 1813 responded to this pressure.

### Main provisions
- **Ended the Company's monopoly of trade with India**. Indian trade was thrown open to all British merchants.
- The Company **kept its monopoly of trade in tea and its trade with China**.
- **Asserted the sovereignty of the British Crown** over the Company's territories in India.
- **Allowed Christian missionaries** to come to India to spread their religion and Western ideas.
- Provided **₹1 lakh a year** for the revival and improvement of literature, the encouragement of learned Indians and the promotion of scientific knowledge. This is regarded as the **first provision of public money for education** in British India.
- Renewed the charter for another **20 years**.

### Significance
The Act marks the shift of British interest from Company trade to India as a **market for British manufactured goods**. The education grant later led to the debate between the **Orientalists** and the **Anglicists** over how the money should be spent.

:::keyfacts
- Trade monopoly ended **except tea and trade with China**
- **₹1 lakh** a year for education
- **Missionaries** permitted to enter India
:::

:::caution
The 1813 Act did **not** end all trade by the Company. The tea and China trade monopoly ended only with the Charter Act of **1833**.
:::

:::exam-tip
Questions often list three or four features and ask which belong to the 1813 Act. Watch for "end of all commercial activities", which belongs to **1833**, not 1813.
:::

## Charter Act of 1833 {#charter-act-1833}

The Charter Act of 1833 is regarded as the **final step towards centralisation** in British India. It was passed in a period of reform in Britain, after the Reform Act of 1832.

### Main provisions
- The **Governor-General of Bengal** became the **Governor-General of India**, with all civil and military powers. **Lord William Bentinck** was the **first Governor-General of India**.
- For the first time, it created a **Government of India** with authority over the whole of British-held territory.
- The Governors of **Bombay and Madras lost their legislative powers**. The Governor-General of India was given **exclusive legislative powers** for the whole of British India.
- Laws made earlier were called **Regulations**; laws made under this Act were called **Acts**.
- **Ended the Company's commercial activities** altogether, including its tea and China trade monopoly. The Company became a **purely administrative body**.
- A **fourth member, the law member**, was added to the Governor-General's council for legislative work. **Thomas Babington Macaulay** was the first law member.
- A **Law Commission** was set up for the **codification of laws**. The first Law Commission, headed by Macaulay, drafted the **Indian Penal Code** (enacted in 1860).
- It laid down that **no Indian should be debarred** from any office under the Company on grounds of religion, place of birth, descent or colour.
- It tried to introduce **open competition** for selecting civil servants, but this provision was not put into effect because of opposition from the Court of Directors.

:::keyfacts
- First Governor-General of India: **Lord William Bentinck**
- First law member: **T.B. Macaulay**
- Company's **commercial activities ended**; it became a purely administrative body
- Regulations → **Acts**
:::

:::caution
Open competition for the civil services was **attempted** in 1833 but actually **introduced** by the Charter Act of **1853**.
:::

## Charter Act of 1853 {#charter-act-1853}

The Charter Act of 1853 was the **last** of the Charter Acts. It was passed during the tenure of **Lord Dalhousie**.

### Main provisions
- **Separated the legislative and executive functions** of the Governor-General's council for the first time.
- **Six new members**, called legislative councillors, were added for legislative work. This enlarged body came to be called the **Indian (Central) Legislative Council**, and it worked somewhat like a small parliament.
- The six new members were the Chief Justice and one other judge of the Supreme Court at Calcutta, and **four representatives of the local (provincial) governments** of Madras, Bombay, Bengal and Agra. This introduced **local representation** in the central legislative council for the first time.
- The **law member** became a full member of the Governor-General's executive council.
- Introduced **open competition** for recruitment to the civil services, which was opened to Indians as well. The Court of Directors lost its **patronage** over these appointments. The **Macaulay Committee** on the Indian Civil Service was appointed in **1854**.
- The number of Directors was reduced from 24 to 18, of whom six were to be nominated by the Crown.
- Unlike earlier charters, it **did not renew the charter for a fixed period of 20 years**. The Company was to keep its territories in trust for the Crown **until Parliament decided otherwise**. This signalled that Company rule could be ended at any time.

:::keyfacts
- **Last** Charter Act
- **Separation** of legislative and executive functions
- **Open competition** for civil services
- **Local representation** in the central legislative council
:::

:::exam-tip
"Mini-parliament", "open competition" and "last Charter Act" all point to **1853**. "Governor-General of India", "law member" and "end of commercial activities" point to **1833**.
:::

## Comparison and the road to Crown rule {#comparison}

The table below sums up the most-asked feature of each Act.

| Act | Key changes | Governor-General at the time |
|---|---|---|
| Regulating Act, 1773 | Governor-General of Bengal with a council of four; Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774); ban on private trade and presents | Warren Hastings (first GG of Bengal) |
| Pitt's India Act, 1784 | Board of Control; dual system; "British possessions in India"; council cut to three | Warren Hastings |
| Act of 1786 | Governor-General may override council and be Commander-in-Chief | Lord Cornwallis |
| Charter Act, 1793 | Charter renewed for 20 years; Board of Control salaries from Indian revenues | — |
| Charter Act, 1813 | Trade monopoly ended except tea and China trade; ₹1 lakh for education; missionaries allowed | — |
| Charter Act, 1833 | Governor-General of India; Company's trade ended; law member; Law Commission | Lord William Bentinck (first GG of India) |
| Charter Act, 1853 | Separation of legislative and executive functions; open competition; local representation; last Charter Act | Lord Dalhousie |

### Link to the Government of India Act, 1858
After the **Revolt of 1857**, the **Government of India Act, 1858** ended Company rule and transferred power to the **British Crown**. It abolished the **Board of Control** and the **Court of Directors**, which ended the dual system of 1784. A **Secretary of State for India** took over their powers, and the Governor-General also became the **Viceroy**, with **Lord Canning** as the first Viceroy. The details are covered in the topic on the Revolt of 1857.

:::mnemonic
**G**overnor-General titles: **B**engal (1773) → **I**ndia (1833) → **V**iceroy (1858). Firsts: **Hastings → Bentinck → Canning**.
:::
