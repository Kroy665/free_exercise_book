# Rise of Nationalism and the Moderates

## Factors Behind Modern Nationalism {#factors}

In the second half of the 19th century, educated Indians began to see themselves as one people with common interests that were being harmed by colonial rule. This awareness grew out of several conditions created, often unintentionally, by British rule itself.

### Main factors
- **Political and administrative unification**: a single administration, uniform laws and a common civil service brought the whole subcontinent under one system of rule.
- **Modern communications**: railways, the telegraph and a unified postal system made it easier for people and ideas to travel between regions.
- **Western education**: English education introduced ideas of liberty, democracy, rationalism and nationalism. English also became a common language for educated Indians from different provinces.
- **The press and literature**: newspapers in English and Indian languages criticised government policy and spread political awareness. Novels, poems and songs encouraged pride in the country.
- **Rediscovery of India's past**: the work of Indian and European scholars on ancient Indian history and texts gave Indians confidence in their own civilisation.
- **Economic exploitation**: a growing body of writing showed how colonial economic policy was making India poor (see the drain theory below).
- **Socio-religious reform movements**: reformers attacked social evils and encouraged free thinking and self-respect.
- **Racial arrogance**: Indians often faced discrimination, especially in courts and public places, and Indian officials were kept out of senior posts.

### Lord Lytton's unpopular measures
The viceroyalty of **Lord Lytton (1876–80)** gave the new political class many reasons for protest:
- An imperial **Delhi Durbar (1877)** was held while large parts of the country suffered a severe famine.
- The **Vernacular Press Act (1878)** placed restrictions on newspapers in Indian languages.
- The **Arms Act (1878)** required Indians, but not Europeans, to hold a licence to keep arms.
- The **maximum age for the Indian Civil Service (ICS) examination was lowered from 21 to 19**, making it even harder for Indians, who had to travel to London for the examination.

:::keyfacts
- Nationalism grew among the **English-educated middle class** first
- Lytton's measures: **Delhi Durbar 1877, Vernacular Press Act 1878, Arms Act 1878, ICS age cut to 19**
- The **Ilbert Bill controversy (1883)** showed Indians the power of organised agitation
:::

:::caution
The **Vernacular Press Act (1878)** was passed under **Lytton** and **repealed in 1882 under Lord Ripon**. Do not confuse the two viceroys.
:::

## Pre-Congress Associations {#associations}

Before 1885, Indians formed a number of political associations. The early ones were dominated by landlords and wealthy people and had mostly regional aims. The later ones were led by the educated middle class and took up wider public questions.

| Association | Year | Place | Founders / leading figures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Landholders' Society | 1838 | Calcutta | Dwarkanath Tagore, Prasanna Kumar Tagore, Radhakanta Deb |
| Bengal British India Society | 1843 | Calcutta | Formed with the help of George Thompson |
| British Indian Association | 1851 | Calcutta | Radhakanta Deb (president), Debendranath Tagore (secretary) |
| East India Association | 1866 | London | Dadabhai Naoroji |
| Poona Sarvajanik Sabha | 1870 (some books: 1867) | Poona | Poona leaders; M.G. Ranade was its guiding figure |
| Indian League | 1875 | Calcutta | Sisir Kumar Ghosh |
| Indian Association | 1876 | Calcutta | Surendranath Banerjea, Ananda Mohan Bose |
| Madras Mahajan Sabha | 1884 | Madras | M. Viraraghavachari, G. Subramania Iyer, P. Ananda Charlu |
| Bombay Presidency Association | 1885 | Bombay | Pherozeshah Mehta, K.T. Telang, Badruddin Tyabji |

### Notes on key associations
- The **Landholders' Society (1838)** is often called the first political association of modern India. It mainly protected the interests of zamindars.
- The **British Indian Association (1851)** was formed by the merger of the Landholders' Society and the Bengal British India Society. It sent petitions to the British Parliament when the Company's charter came up for renewal in 1853.
- The **East India Association (1866)** was set up by **Dadabhai Naoroji in London** to place Indian questions before the British public and Parliament.
- The **Poona Sarvajanik Sabha** is usually dated to **1870**; some books give **1867**. Books also list its founders differently, but **M.G. Ranade** is widely regarded as its guiding figure. It acted as a link between the government and the people of the region.

### The Indian Association (1876)
The **Indian Association** was founded in **Calcutta in 1876** by **Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose**. It is regarded as the most important pre-Congress association because it aimed at all-India political work and at drawing in the middle class.
- It organised a country-wide agitation against the **lowering of the ICS age limit** and demanded simultaneous ICS examinations in India and England.
- It campaigned against the Arms Act and the Vernacular Press Act.
- It held an all-India **Indian National Conference** in Calcutta in **1883** and again in **1885**. The Conference merged with the Indian National Congress in **1886**.

:::exam-tip
WBCS often asks about Bengal's associations. Remember the order: **Landholders' Society (1838) → Bengal British India Society (1843) → British Indian Association (1851) → Indian Association (1876)**.
:::

## The Ilbert Bill Controversy {#ilbert-bill}

The Ilbert Bill showed educated Indians how deep racial prejudice ran, and also how effective organised agitation could be.

### Background
In criminal cases in the districts, a European could not be tried by an Indian judge or magistrate. By the early 1880s some Indians had risen to senior judicial posts, so this rule was both unfair to them and inconvenient.

### The Bill (1883)
- In **1883**, during the viceroyalty of **Lord Ripon**, the Law Member **C.P. Ilbert** introduced a bill to remove this distinction.
- It would have allowed qualified **Indian judges to try European offenders** in criminal cases.

### Opposition and compromise
- Europeans in India, especially planters and businessmen, organised a fierce protest. They raised funds, held meetings and used the press to attack the Bill. This campaign is sometimes called the **"White Mutiny"**.
- The government gave in and passed a much weaker version in **1884**. A European accused could now **claim trial by a jury, at least half of whose members had to be Europeans**.

### Significance
- Indians saw that the British in India would not accept equality with Indians.
- The success of the Europeans' organised campaign showed that united, well-planned agitation could influence the government. This lesson encouraged Indians to build an all-India political organisation.

### Lord Ripon's other measures
Ripon is remembered for other liberal steps as well: he **repealed the Vernacular Press Act (1882)**, issued a resolution on **local self-government (1882)** and appointed the **Hunter Commission (1882)** on education.

:::keyfacts
- Ilbert Bill: **1883**, Viceroy **Ripon**, Law Member **C.P. Ilbert**
- Aim: allow Indian judges to try **Europeans** in criminal cases
- Result: diluted in **1884**; Europeans could demand a jury with **at least half Europeans**
:::

:::caution
The Ilbert Bill was **not withdrawn**; it was passed in a **diluted form** in 1884.
:::

## Founding of the Indian National Congress {#inc-founding}

The idea of an all-India organisation had been growing for some years. It took shape in December 1885.

### The first session
- **A.O. Hume**, a retired British officer of the ICS, worked with Indian leaders to bring delegates together. He became the Congress's **general secretary**.
- The body was first planned as the **Indian National Union**; its name was changed to the **Indian National Congress**.
- The session was planned for **Poona**, but because of an outbreak of **cholera** there, it was moved to **Bombay**.
- It met on **28 December 1885** at the **Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay**.
- **72 delegates** attended.
- **Womesh Chandra (W.C.) Bonnerjee** presided.
- The Viceroy at the time was **Lord Dufferin**.

### Aims stated by the early Congress
- To develop friendly ties among nationalist workers from different parts of the country.
- To build a feeling of national unity, cutting across region, caste and religion.
- To frame popular demands and present them to the government.
- To train and organise public opinion in the country.

### The "safety valve" debate
Historians do not agree on why Hume helped form the Congress.
- **The safety-valve view**: according to this view, Hume wanted the Congress to act as a "safety valve" that would release growing popular discontent harmlessly and so protect British rule. The idea is often traced to **William Wedderburn's** biography of Hume. Later, **Lala Lajpat Rai** used it in his book *Young India* (1916) to criticise the Moderates, and the Marxist writer **R. Palme Dutt** held a similar view.
- **The "lightning conductor" view**: **Bipan Chandra** and other historians reject the safety-valve view as too simple. They argue that Indian leaders wanted an all-India body of their own and accepted Hume's help so that the new body would not be treated as hostile by the government. In this reading, Hume served as a **"lightning conductor"** for the Indian leaders.

:::keyfacts
- First session: **28 December 1885**, **Bombay**, Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College
- President: **W.C. Bonnerjee**; delegates: **72**
- Organiser: **A.O. Hume** (retired ICS officer); Viceroy: **Dufferin**
:::

:::exam-tip
Questions on the safety-valve theory test **who held which view**, not whether the theory is true. Link Lajpat Rai with the safety-valve view and Bipan Chandra with the "lightning conductor" view.
:::

## Early Sessions and Presidents {#early-sessions}

After 1885 the Congress met once a year, in the last week of December, at a different city each time. This helped it reach educated people in all the provinces.

| Year | Place | President | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1885 | Bombay | W.C. Bonnerjee | First session |
| 1886 | Calcutta | Dadabhai Naoroji | Indian National Conference merged with the Congress |
| 1887 | Madras | Badruddin Tyabji | First Muslim president |
| 1888 | Allahabad | George Yule | First English (non-Indian) president |
| 1889 | Bombay | William Wedderburn | |
| 1890 | Calcutta | Pherozeshah Mehta | |

### Points to note
- **Dadabhai Naoroji** presided three times: **1886 (Calcutta), 1893 (Lahore) and 1906 (Calcutta)**.
- **A.O. Hume** was the general secretary, but he **never presided** over a Congress session.
- The early sessions were attended mainly by lawyers, journalists, teachers, doctors and merchants, that is, the educated middle class.

:::mnemonic
**"B-N-T-Y"** for 1885–1888: **B**onnerjee (Bombay), **N**aoroji (Calcutta), **T**yabji (Madras), **Y**ule (Allahabad).
:::

:::caution
**George Yule (1888)** was the first English president. **William Wedderburn** presided the next year, in **1889**. Hume, though a founder, was never president.
:::

## Moderate Leaders and Their Methods {#moderate-leaders}

The period from **1885 to 1905** is usually called the **Moderate phase** of the national movement. Its leaders are called the **Moderates** (or early nationalists).

### Leading Moderates
- **Dadabhai Naoroji**: known as the **"Grand Old Man of India"**. Founded the East India Association, wrote on Indian poverty, and in **1892** became the **first Indian elected to the British House of Commons**.
- **Surendranath Banerjea**: founder of the Indian Association; a leading speaker and organiser in Bengal.
- **Pherozeshah Mehta**: a leading figure of Bombay politics and of the Bombay Presidency Association.
- **Gopal Krishna Gokhale**: regarded M.G. Ranade as his teacher; founded the **Servants of India Society (1905)**. Gandhi regarded Gokhale as his political guru.
- **M.G. Ranade**, **W.C. Bonnerjee**, **Badruddin Tyabji**, **Ananda Mohan Bose**, **R.C. Dutt** and **Dinshaw Wacha** were also important.

### Beliefs
- Faith in the **British sense of justice and fair play**, and the belief that British rule had some benefits, such as modern education and communications.
- The view that Indians were not yet fully ready for self-rule and that change should come **step by step**.
- Commitment to **constitutional and peaceful methods**.

### Methods: prayer, petition and protest
- Passing resolutions at Congress sessions.
- Sending **petitions and memorials** to the government and the British Parliament.
- Holding meetings and writing in the press to educate public opinion in India.
- Making Indian demands known **in Britain**: a **British Committee of the Congress** worked in London, and leaders like Dadabhai Naoroji spoke to British audiences.

:::keyfacts
- Moderate phase: **1885–1905**
- Methods: **prayer, petition, protest** (constitutional agitation)
- "Grand Old Man of India": **Dadabhai Naoroji**
- First Indian in the British House of Commons: **Dadabhai Naoroji (1892)**
:::

:::caution
The term "political mendicancy" (begging) was a **criticism** of the Moderates' methods made by their opponents. It is not a name the Moderates used for themselves.
:::

## Demands of the Moderates {#demands}

The Moderates' demands were practical and limited. They wanted reforms within British rule, not an immediate end to it.

### Economic critique
The Moderates' most lasting work was their **economic critique of colonialism**.
- **Dadabhai Naoroji** put forward the **"drain theory"**: a part of India's wealth was being transferred to Britain without any equal return, through items such as home charges, salaries and pensions of British officials, and profits sent abroad. His book is **"Poverty and Un-British Rule in India"**.
- **R.C. Dutt** wrote the **"Economic History of India"**. **M.G. Ranade** also wrote on Indian economics.
- They blamed colonial policy for India's poverty, the decline of handicrafts and repeated famines.

Economic demands included:
- Reduction of **land revenue** and protection of peasants.
- Cuts in **military expenditure** and in the **home charges**.
- Development of **modern Indian industry**, with protection against unequal competition.
- Abolition or reduction of the **salt tax**.

### Constitutional demands
- **Expansion and reform of the legislative councils**, with more elected Indian members and greater powers, such as discussing and voting on the budget.
- Their long-term aim was **self-government** within the British Empire, to be reached gradually.

### Administrative demands
- **Simultaneous ICS examinations in India and England**, and a higher age limit for the examination, so that more Indians could enter the service. (A resolution of the British House of Commons favoured simultaneous examinations in 1893, but it was not put into effect.)
- **Indianisation of the higher services**.
- **Separation of the judiciary from the executive**.
- Wider spending on education, health and irrigation.

### Civil rights
- Freedom of **speech, press and association**, and opposition to measures that restricted them.

:::exam-tip
UPSC likes questions that link a book with its author: **Poverty and Un-British Rule in India → Dadabhai Naoroji**; **Economic History of India → R.C. Dutt**.
:::

## Indian Councils Act 1892 {#councils-act-1892}

The Moderates' demand for council reform produced the **Indian Councils Act of 1892**, passed during the viceroyalty of **Lord Lansdowne**.

### Main provisions
- The number of **additional (non-official) members** in the central (Imperial) and provincial legislative councils was **increased**, but the **official majority** was kept.
- Members could **discuss the annual budget**, but they **could not vote** on it.
- Members could **ask questions** on matters of public interest, after giving notice. They could not ask supplementary questions.
- Some non-official members were to be **nominated on the recommendation** of bodies such as provincial councils, district boards, municipalities, universities, chambers of commerce and associations of zamindars. The word "election" was avoided, but this introduced a limited, **indirect element of election**.

### The Moderates' response
- The Moderates welcomed the principle of representation but were **dissatisfied**. The councils remained advisory, and officials kept a majority.
- They continued to demand a larger elected element and real control over finance.

:::keyfacts
- Act: **1892**, Viceroy **Lansdowne**
- Budget: could be **discussed, not voted on**
- Questions allowed, **no supplementary questions**
- **Indirect, limited** element of election through recommending bodies
:::

:::caution
The right to **vote on the budget** did not come in 1892. The 1892 Act allowed only **discussion** of the budget.
:::

## Assessment of the Moderates {#assessment}

Historians judge the Moderates in different ways. Their critics, especially the Extremists of the early 20th century, stressed their limited success. Many later historians stress the groundwork they laid.

### Achievements
- They created a **national political platform** and brought together leaders from all regions.
- Their **economic critique** exposed the exploitative side of colonial rule and weakened the claim that British rule was for India's good.
- They trained Indians in **public political work** and popularised ideas of democracy, civil liberties and representative government.
- Their demands led to some reforms, such as the **Indian Councils Act 1892**.

### Limitations
- Their **social base was narrow**: mainly the urban, English-educated middle class. Peasants, workers and women were largely outside the movement.
- Their **faith in British justice** proved over-optimistic; most of their demands were not met.
- Their methods were criticised as too mild. In 1893, **Aurobindo Ghose** criticised the Congress in a series of articles titled **"New Lamps for Old"**.

### The road ahead
By the early 1900s, a new group of leaders, the **Extremists**, wanted more direct methods and broader public participation. The differences between the two groups came to a head after the partition of Bengal (1905), covered in the next topic.

:::exam-tip
In descriptive questions, give a **balanced assessment**: the Moderates' gains were limited, but they built the political and economic foundations on which the later movement stood.
:::
