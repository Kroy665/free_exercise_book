# Advent of the Europeans

## Background {#background}

From ancient times India traded with Europe through West Asia. Spices, pepper, cotton textiles and indigo reached Europe by land and sea routes controlled by Arab and Italian (Venetian and Genoese) merchants. After the **Ottoman Turks captured Constantinople in 1453**, the old routes became costlier and less secure. Western European states, helped by advances in shipbuilding and navigation, began searching for a direct **sea route to India**.

The **Portuguese** led the way under royal patronage. Their voyages down the African coast ended with the passage round the **Cape of Good Hope**, which opened the all-sea route to the Indian Ocean. Later the Dutch, the English, the Danes and the French formed trading companies to share in the profitable Asian trade.

Unlike the Portuguese venture, which was run by the Crown, these were **chartered companies**: each government granted its company a monopoly of trade with the East. The companies set up **factories** (trading posts with warehouses, often fortified; not manufacturing units) along the coasts, competed fiercely with one another and gradually began to interfere in Indian politics.

| Power | Company / start | Main early settlements |
|---|---|---|
| Portuguese | State enterprise; Vasco da Gama, **1498** | Goa, Cochin, Daman, Diu, Hooghly |
| Dutch | **VOC, 1602** | Masulipatnam, Pulicat, Chinsura, Surat |
| English | **East India Company, 1600** | Surat, Madras, Bombay, Calcutta |
| Danish | **Danish East India Company, 1616** | Tranquebar, Serampore |
| French | **Compagnie des Indes Orientales, 1664** | Pondicherry, Chandernagore, Mahe, Karaikal |

:::caution
Textbooks usually list the arrivals as **Portuguese → Dutch → English → Danes → French**. By date of company formation, however, the English East India Company (**1600**) is older than the Dutch VOC (**1602**). Read the question carefully: "arrival" and "formation of the company" can give different answers.
:::

:::exam-tip
A "factory" in this period means a **trading station**, not a manufacturing unit. This is a common SSC and Railways question.
:::

## The Portuguese {#portuguese}

The Portuguese were the **first Europeans to reach India by sea** and the **last to leave**.

### Arrival
- **Vasco da Gama** reached **Calicut** on the Malabar coast in **May 1498** after sailing round the Cape of Good Hope. The local ruler, the **Zamorin**, received him.
- **Pedro Álvares Cabral** led the next Portuguese voyage to Calicut in **1500**. Vasco da Gama came again in **1502**.
- Early Portuguese trading posts came up at **Cochin** and **Cannanore**.

### Builders of the Portuguese empire
- **Francisco de Almeida** (1505–1509), the **first Portuguese viceroy** in India, followed the **"Blue Water Policy"**: Portugal should be supreme at sea rather than build land forts. In the **naval battle of Diu (1509)** he defeated a combined fleet of Gujarat, Egypt and the Zamorin.
- **Afonso de Albuquerque** (1509–1515), regarded as the **real founder of Portuguese power** in India, **captured Goa from the Sultan of Bijapur in 1510**. He also took **Malacca (1511)** and **Hormuz (1515)**, gaining control of key points on the Indian Ocean routes. He encouraged Portuguese men to marry Indian women and is credited with prohibiting sati in Goa.
- **Nino da Cunha** moved the Portuguese headquarters from **Cochin to Goa in 1530**. Bassein and Diu were acquired from Gujarat in the 1530s.

### The cartaz system
The Portuguese claimed control of the Arabian Sea. Asian merchant ships had to buy a **cartaz** (a naval pass or licence) from Portuguese authorities. Ships without one could be seized. This **cartaz–armada–cafila** system let the Portuguese tax and control sea trade.

### Decline
- Shah Jahan's forces took **Hooghly** from the Portuguese in **1632**.
- **Bombay** passed to the English Crown in **1661** as part of the dowry of the Portuguese princess **Catherine of Braganza** on her marriage to **Charles II**.
- Religious intolerance, dependence on force, and competition from the Dutch and English weakened them. They kept only **Goa, Daman and Diu**, which became part of India in **1961**.

:::keyfacts
- First European to reach India by sea: **Vasco da Gama, Calicut, 1498**
- First viceroy: **Almeida** (Blue Water Policy)
- Capture of Goa: **Albuquerque, 1510**
- The Portuguese brought **tobacco** cultivation to India and set up a **printing press in Goa (1556)**.
:::

## The Dutch {#dutch}

The Dutch (Hollanders) were mainly interested in the **spice islands of Indonesia**. India mattered to them chiefly as a source of **cotton textiles**, which they exchanged for spices in South-East Asia.

### Company and factories
- The **United East India Company of the Netherlands** (Dutch initials **VOC**) was formed in **1602** with the power to make war, conclude treaties, acquire territory and build forts.
- **First Dutch factory in India: Masulipatnam (1605)** on the Andhra coast.
- **Pulicat**, north of Madras, became their main centre on the Coromandel coast; it was later replaced by **Negapatam**.
- Other factories: **Surat**, **Chinsura** (Bengal), **Kasimbazar**, **Patna**, **Balasore** and **Cochin**.
- Main exports from India: textiles, indigo, saltpetre, raw silk and rice.

### Rivalry with the English
- The Dutch pushed the Portuguese out of the spice trade and became the strongest European power in the East Indies.
- In the **Amboyna massacre (1623)** the Dutch killed English traders in the East Indies. After this the English concentrated more on India.
- The Dutch challenge in Bengal ended at the **Battle of Bedara (1759)**, where the English defeated them.

:::caution
Do not confuse **Chinsura** (Dutch) with **Chandernagore** (French) and **Serampore** (Danish). All three were on the Hooghly river in Bengal.
:::

:::exam-tip
Remember the pair: **first Dutch factory = Masulipatnam (1605)**; **final Dutch defeat by the English = Bedara (1759)**.
:::

## The English East India Company {#english}

### Formation
- On **31 December 1600**, **Queen Elizabeth I** granted a charter to the "Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies", popularly called the **East India Company**. It got a monopoly of English trade with the East, initially for **15 years**.
- The Company was a **private joint-stock company** owned by merchants, not by the state.

### Gaining a foothold in western India
- **Captain William Hawkins** reached the court of **Jahangir** in **1609** seeking permission to trade at Surat. Portuguese pressure at the court prevented success.
- In the **Battle of Swally (Suvali), 1612**, near Surat, an English fleet under **Captain Thomas Best** defeated the Portuguese. This raised English prestige at the Mughal court.
- In **1613** Jahangir allowed the English to set up a **permanent factory at Surat**.
- **Sir Thomas Roe**, ambassador of **King James I**, stayed at Jahangir's court from **1615** and obtained permission to trade and set up factories in different parts of the empire.

### The three presidencies
| Presidency | How it began |
|---|---|
| **Madras** | **Francis Day** obtained a grant of land from the local Raja of Chandragiri in **1639**; **Fort St. George** was built there. |
| **Bombay** | Received by Charles II as dowry (1661) and **leased to the Company in 1668** for a nominal rent of £10 a year. The western headquarters moved from **Surat to Bombay in 1687**. |
| **Calcutta** | **Job Charnock** set up a settlement at **Sutanati** in **1690**. In **1698** the Company acquired the zamindari of **Sutanati, Kalikata and Gobindapur**; the fort built there was named **Fort William**. |

- In the east, the English set up a factory at **Masulipatnam (1611)** and, in Bengal, at **Hooghly (1651)**.
- In **1717** the Mughal emperor **Farrukhsiyar** issued a **farman** allowing the Company to trade in Bengal free of customs duties on payment of a fixed annual sum. It is sometimes called the Company's "Magna Carta".

:::timeline
- 1600 | Charter from Elizabeth I
- 1609 | Hawkins at Jahangir's court
- 1612 | Battle of Swally
- 1613 | Permanent factory at Surat
- 1615 | Sir Thomas Roe at the Mughal court
- 1639 | Madras (Fort St. George)
- 1668 | Bombay leased to the Company
- 1690 | Job Charnock at Sutanati
- 1717 | Farman of Farrukhsiyar
:::

## The Danes and the French {#danes-french}

### The Danes
- The **Danish East India Company** was formed in **1616**.
- Settlements: **Tranquebar** (Tamil Nadu coast, 1620) and **Serampore** (Bengal).
- Serampore became known for the missionary work and printing press of the Baptist missionary **William Carey**.
- The Danes were a minor commercial power. They **sold their Indian settlements to the English in 1845**.

### The French
- The **French East India Company** (*Compagnie des Indes Orientales*) was set up in **1664** during the reign of **Louis XIV**, at the initiative of his minister **Colbert**.
- It was a **state-controlled** company: the French government funded it, appointed its directors and supervised its affairs.
- **First French factory: Surat (1668)**, set up under **François Caron**. A second factory came up at **Masulipatnam (1669)**.
- **Pondicherry** was founded in **1674** by **François Martin** and became the French headquarters in India.
- Other settlements: **Chandernagore** (Bengal), **Mahe** (Malabar coast), **Karaikal** and **Yanam**.
- **Joseph François Dupleix** became governor of Pondicherry in **1742**. He pioneered the policy of building political power by **taking sides in disputes between Indian rulers**, using Indian soldiers (sepoys) trained in European methods. This brought the French into direct conflict with the English.

:::caution
The first French factory was at **Surat (1668)**, not Pondicherry. Pondicherry was founded later, in **1674**.
:::

:::mnemonic
Order of **arrival**: **P**ortuguese, **D**utch, **E**nglish, **D**anes, **F**rench — "**P**lease **D**o **E**at **D**inner **F**irst." For **company formation** only the first pair changes: English (1600) before Dutch (1602).
:::

## The Carnatic Wars {#carnatic-wars}

The Carnatic (the Coromandel coast and its hinterland) saw three wars between the English and the French. They were linked to wars between Britain and France in Europe and to succession disputes among Indian rulers.

### First Carnatic War (1746–1748)
- An extension of the **War of the Austrian Succession** in Europe.
- The French under **La Bourdonnais** captured **Madras** in 1746.
- At the **Battle of St. Thomé** (on the Adyar river), a small French force defeated the much larger army of **Anwaruddin, the Nawab of the Carnatic**. It showed that a small, disciplined European-trained force could defeat a large Indian army.
- Ended by the **Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)**; **Madras was returned to the English**.

### Second Carnatic War (1749–1754)
- Arose from **succession disputes** in Hyderabad and the Carnatic. Dupleix backed **Muzaffar Jang** (Hyderabad) and **Chanda Sahib** (Carnatic); the English backed **Nasir Jang** and **Muhammad Ali**.
- Anwaruddin was killed at the **Battle of Ambur (1749)**.
- **Robert Clive** captured and defended **Arcot (1751)**, which turned the war in favour of the English.
- Dupleix was **recalled to France in 1754** and replaced by **Godeheu**. The war ended with a treaty at Pondicherry; Muhammad Ali remained Nawab of the Carnatic.

### Third Carnatic War (1756–1763)
- Part of the **Seven Years' War** in Europe. Serious fighting in the Carnatic began only in 1758, when Lally arrived, so some books date the war 1758–1763.
- The French government sent **Count de Lally**. **Bussy** was called back from Hyderabad to help him.
- **Battle of Wandiwash (22 January 1760)**: the English under **Sir Eyre Coote** decisively defeated Lally; Bussy was taken prisoner.
- The English captured **Pondicherry in 1761**.
- The **Treaty of Paris (1763)** returned Pondicherry and other French settlements, but only as trading posts that could **not be fortified**.

:::keyfacts
- 1st war: Battle of St. Thomé; Treaty of **Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)**
- 2nd war: **Clive at Arcot (1751)**; recall of **Dupleix (1754)**
- 3rd war: **Wandiwash, 1760** (Eyre Coote vs Lally); Treaty of **Paris (1763)**
:::

:::exam-tip
The **Battle of Wandiwash** ended the French bid for power in India. Do not confuse it with **Plassey (1757)**, which was fought against the Nawab of Bengal, Siraj-ud-daulah, not the French.
:::

## Why the English won {#why-english-won}

The English emerged as the only serious European power in India by the 1760s. Historians point to several reasons.

| Factor | English | French |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of company | Private company; merchants had a direct stake in profit and acted quickly | State-controlled; decisions depended on a distant government |
| Navy | **Stronger navy**; could bring men and supplies from Europe and cut off French supplies | Weaker at sea |
| Resources | Revenue and trade of **Bengal after Plassey (1757)** paid for the wars in the south | Depended on the Carnatic and on money from France |
| Bases | Three strong centres: **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras** | Only **Pondicherry** as a major base |
| Leadership | Clive, Stringer Lawrence, Eyre Coote | Able men (Dupleix, Bussy) but quarrels among leaders (Lally and others) |
| Home policy | Britain gave priority to trade and the colonies | France was more absorbed in continental European wars |

- The English Company kept its trade going even in wartime, so it had money to pay its troops. The French Company often depended on the French government for funds.
- The **recall of Dupleix (1754)** weakened French policy at a crucial moment.

:::exam-tip
UPSC-style questions often ask for the **"most important"** reason. The two factors usually stressed in standard texts are **naval superiority** and the **resources of Bengal** after Plassey.
:::
