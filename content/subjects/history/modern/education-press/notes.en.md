# Education, Press and the Indian Middle Class

## Early institutions and the Orientalist phase {#early-institutions}

In its first decades of rule, the East India Company did not see the education of Indians as its duty. A few officials, however, believed that the Company would govern better if it respected and studied India's classical languages, laws and religious texts. This approach is called **Orientalism**, and its supporters are known as **Orientalists**. They also needed Indians trained in Persian, Arabic and Sanskrit learning to help in courts and revenue work.

### Major early institutions
- **Calcutta Madrasa (1781)**: set up by **Warren Hastings** for the study of Muslim law and related subjects in Arabic and Persian.
- **Asiatic Society of Bengal (1784)**: founded in Calcutta by **Sir William Jones**, a judge of the Supreme Court and a scholar of Sanskrit. It promoted research into India's history, languages and antiquities.
- **Sanskrit College at Benares (1791)**: set up by **Jonathan Duncan**, the British Resident at Benares, for the study of Hindu law and philosophy.
- **Fort William College (1800)**: founded in Calcutta by **Lord Wellesley** to train young British civil servants in Indian languages and customs. It also encouraged the writing of prose and textbooks in Indian languages, including Bengali.

### Missionaries and private efforts
- Christian missionaries, especially **William Carey** and his colleagues at **Serampore** near Calcutta (from 1800), printed books in Indian languages and opened schools.
- **Hindu College (1817)** in Calcutta was set up with the support of leading Calcutta citizens and **David Hare** to give Western-style education to Indian boys. It later became Presidency College.

:::keyfacts
- Calcutta Madrasa: **1781**, Warren Hastings
- Asiatic Society: **1784**, William Jones
- Sanskrit College, Benares: **1791**, Jonathan Duncan
- Fort William College: **1800**, Lord Wellesley
:::

:::caution
Fort William College was meant to train **British** officials, not to educate Indians. Do not confuse it with Hindu College (1817), which taught Indian students.
:::

:::mnemonic
**M-A-S-F** in order of founding: **M**adrasa (1781) → **A**siatic Society (1784) → **S**anskrit College (1791) → **F**ort William College (1800).
:::

## Charter Act 1813 and the Orientalist–Anglicist debate {#orientalist-anglicist}

The **Charter Act of 1813** was the first time the British Parliament asked the Company to spend public money on education. It set aside **₹1 lakh a year** for the revival and improvement of literature, the encouragement of learned Indians and the introduction and promotion of knowledge of the sciences. The Act did not say which language or kind of learning the money should support, and for years little of it was spent.

In **1823** the Company set up the **General Committee of Public Instruction** to manage the education grant. Its members soon split into two groups.

| | Orientalists | Anglicists |
|---|---|---|
| Wanted | Support for traditional learning in Sanskrit, Arabic and Persian | Western learning taught through the English language |
| Typical demand | More grants to oriental colleges and to printing classical texts | Grants to English schools and colleges |
| Known supporters | H.T. Prinsep and many senior officials of the committee | T.B. Macaulay, Charles Trevelyan |

Many educated Indians also wanted modern education. In **1823 Raja Rammohun Roy** wrote to the Governor-General, **Lord Amherst**, protesting against the plan for a new Sanskrit college in Calcutta and asking the government to teach mathematics, natural science and other modern subjects instead.

### The vernacular question
A third view, held by some officials, favoured teaching Western knowledge through **Indian languages (vernaculars)**. It received less support at the time, but it returned in Wood's Despatch of 1854.

:::keyfacts
- **₹1 lakh** a year for education: **Charter Act of 1813**
- General Committee of Public Instruction: **1823**
- Rammohun Roy's letter to Lord Amherst in favour of Western science: **1823**
:::

:::caution
The 1813 Act provided money but did **not** choose English as the medium. That choice came only in **1835**, with Macaulay's Minute and Bentinck's resolution.
:::

:::exam-tip
Questions may ask you to classify people as Orientalist or Anglicist. Macaulay and Trevelyan are the safest Anglicist names; H.T. Prinsep is the best-known Orientalist on the committee.
:::

## Macaulay's Minute and Bentinck's resolution, 1835 {#macaulay-minute}

**Thomas Babington Macaulay** came to India in 1834 as the first **law member** of the Governor-General's council, a post created by the Charter Act of 1833. As president of the General Committee of Public Instruction, he wrote his **Minute on Indian Education**, dated **2 February 1835**, to settle the Orientalist–Anglicist dispute.

### Main arguments of the Minute (in summary)
- Macaulay was openly dismissive of Sanskrit and Arabic learning. He argued that Western literature and science were far more useful than what the oriental colleges taught.
- He held that the 1813 grant could lawfully be spent on English education, because the Act did not name any language.
- He proposed creating a **small class of English-educated Indians** who would act as interpreters between the rulers and the ruled, sharing English tastes and ideas while remaining Indian by birth.
- This class was expected to spread Western knowledge further among the people. This idea is known as the **downward filtration theory**: education would "filter down" from a few at the top to the masses.

### Bentinck's resolution
Governor-General **Lord William Bentinck** accepted Macaulay's view. His government's resolution of **7 March 1835** declared that the funds for education should be spent mainly on **English education**, that is, on teaching Western literature and science through the English language. Existing oriental institutions were not closed, but new spending went mostly to English schools and colleges.

### Results
- English became the **medium of higher education** in British India.
- Government jobs increasingly went to people with English education, which raised demand for it.
- Mass education in Indian languages was largely neglected for the next two decades.
- In **1849**, **J.E.D. Bethune** set up a school for girls in Calcutta (later Bethune School), an early step in women's education with support from reformers such as **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar**.

:::keyfacts
- Macaulay's Minute: **2 February 1835**
- Bentinck's resolution on English education: **7 March 1835**
- Policy: **downward filtration** through an English-educated few
:::

:::caution
Macaulay was the **law member**, not the Governor-General. The decision was formally taken by **Lord William Bentinck**'s government.
:::

## Wood's Despatch, 1854 and the first universities {#woods-despatch}

By the 1850s the British Government felt the need for a single, planned education policy for India. In **1854**, **Sir Charles Wood**, the **President of the Board of Control**, sent a long despatch on education to the Government of India, then headed by **Lord Dalhousie**. Because it laid down a complete scheme for the first time, **Wood's Despatch** is often called the **"Magna Carta of English Education in India"**.

### Main recommendations
- The government should accept **responsibility for the education of the masses**. In principle this set aside the downward filtration theory.
- A graded system: **vernacular primary schools** in villages, **Anglo-vernacular high schools** and affiliated **colleges** at the district level, and **universities** at the top.
- **Indian languages** as the medium at the primary level, and **English** at the higher levels.
- **Universities** at **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras** on the model of the **University of London**, which was then mainly an examining body.
- A **Department of Public Instruction** in each province.
- A system of **grants-in-aid** to encourage private schools and colleges.
- **Training of teachers**, more attention to **women's education**, and encouragement of **technical and vocational** education.
- Education in government institutions was to be **secular**.

### Universities of 1857
Following the Despatch, the universities of **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras** were set up in **1857**, under Governor-General **Lord Canning**. Like London University, they were **affiliating and examining** bodies; teaching was done in the colleges attached to them. Their first graduates soon formed the core of an English-educated middle class.

:::keyfacts
- Wood's Despatch: **1854**; Charles Wood, President of the Board of Control
- Governor-General at the time: **Lord Dalhousie**
- "Magna Carta of English Education in India"
- First three universities: **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, 1857**
:::

:::caution
Wood's Despatch did **not** make English the only medium. It wanted Indian languages at the primary stage and English at the higher stages.
:::

:::exam-tip
Remember the pairing: **Wood's Despatch 1854 → universities 1857**. A question naming 1857 and "universities" points to these three presidency towns, not to Allahabad or Punjab.
:::

## Hunter Commission, Raleigh Commission and the Act of 1904 {#later-commissions}

After the Despatch of 1854, the government appointed commissions to review how its policy was working.

### Hunter Commission, 1882
- Appointed by **Lord Ripon** in **1882**, under the chairmanship of **W.W. Hunter**.
- Its main task was to review progress since Wood's Despatch, especially in **primary and secondary education**.
- It stressed that the state should take **special care of primary education** and that primary education should be taught in Indian languages.
- It recommended transferring the control of primary education to the new **district and municipal boards** (part of Ripon's local self-government scheme).
- It suggested two streams in secondary education: a **literary** stream leading to university and a **vocational** stream for practical and commercial careers.
- It drew attention to the lack of facilities for **female education**, especially outside the presidency towns.
- New universities followed soon after: **Punjab University (1882)** and **Allahabad University (1887)**.

### Raleigh Commission, 1902 and the Indian Universities Act, 1904
- **Lord Curzon** believed that the universities had lowered standards and had become centres of political discontent. In **1902** he appointed the **Universities Commission**, headed by **Sir Thomas Raleigh**, to report on the condition of universities.
- On its recommendations, the **Indian Universities Act, 1904** was passed.
- Universities were asked to give more attention to **teaching and research**, not only examinations.
- The number of **fellows** (members of the senate) was limited, and **most were to be nominated by the government**.
- The government got the power to **veto regulations** passed by the senate and could make regulations itself.
- **Stricter conditions** were laid down for affiliating private colleges.

### Nationalist response
Nationalists saw the 1904 Act as an attempt to bring the universities under **official control** and to restrict the spread of higher education among Indians. Gopal Krishna Gokhale opposed the bill in the Imperial Legislative Council.

| Commission / Act | Year | Viceroy | Main focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood's Despatch | 1854 | Dalhousie (Governor-General) | Complete scheme of education; universities |
| Hunter Commission | 1882 | Ripon | Primary and secondary education |
| Raleigh Commission | 1902 | Curzon | Universities |
| Indian Universities Act | 1904 | Curzon | Government control over universities |

:::caution
The Hunter Commission was about **primary and secondary** education. The Raleigh Commission dealt with **universities**. Do not swap them.
:::

## Early press and its regulation {#early-press}

Printing presses and newspapers arrived in India with Europeans. The earliest papers were run by Englishmen and often clashed with the Company's officials.

### The first newspaper
- **James Augustus Hicky** started **The Bengal Gazette**, also called the **Calcutta General Advertiser**, in **1780**. It is regarded as the **first newspaper published in India**.
- Hicky's paper attacked senior officials, including Warren Hastings. His press was **seized in 1782**.

### Early Indian-owned papers
- **Raja Rammohun Roy** published **Sambad Kaumudi** in **Bengali (1821)** and **Mirat-ul-Akbar** in **Persian (1822)**. They discussed social, religious and political questions and helped create Indian public opinion.

### Press laws before 1857
- **Censorship of Press Act, 1799**: passed under **Lord Wellesley** because of fears of a French invasion. Newspapers had to be checked by the government before publication (pre-censorship).
- **Licensing Regulations, 1823**: issued by the acting Governor-General **John Adam**. Starting or running a press without a government licence became an offence. Rammohun Roy stopped publishing **Mirat-ul-Akbar** in protest.
- **Press Act, 1835 (Metcalfe's Act)**: the acting Governor-General **Sir Charles Metcalfe** repealed the 1823 regulations. A printer or publisher only had to declare the location of the press. For this liberal law Metcalfe is called the **"liberator of the Indian press"**.
- **Licensing Act, 1857**: during the Revolt of 1857, licensing was brought back for a limited period.
- **Registration Act, 1867**: replaced the 1835 Act. It was a regulatory law: every book and newspaper had to carry the names of the printer and publisher and the place of publication.

:::timeline
- 1780 | Hicky's Bengal Gazette, first newspaper in India
- 1799 | Censorship of Press Act (Wellesley)
- 1823 | Licensing Regulations (John Adam)
- 1835 | Press Act (Metcalfe), "liberator of the Indian press"
- 1857 | Licensing Act during the Revolt
- 1867 | Registration Act
- 1878 | Vernacular Press Act (Lytton)
- 1882 | Vernacular Press Act repealed (Ripon)
:::

:::caution
**Metcalfe** freed the press in **1835**; **John Adam** restricted it in **1823**. Metcalfe and Adam both served only as **acting** Governors-General.
:::

## Vernacular Press Act, 1878 {#vernacular-press-act}

By the 1870s, newspapers in Indian languages were openly criticising British policies. The **great famine of 1876–78** and the costly **Delhi Durbar of 1877**, held while people were dying of hunger, drew especially sharp comment.

### The Act
- The **Vernacular Press Act** was passed in **1878** under Viceroy **Lord Lytton**.
- It applied **only to newspapers in Indian languages**, not to English-language papers.
- The **district magistrate** could ask the printer and publisher of a vernacular paper to sign a **bond** promising not to publish material that might excite disaffection against the government or hostility among different groups.
- If the paper broke this promise, the security could be **forfeited** and the press equipment seized.
- The magistrate's action was **final**. There was **no right of appeal to a court of law**.
- A paper could escape these measures by submitting its proofs to a government censor before publication.

Because it discriminated against Indian-language papers and silenced criticism, Indians called it the **"Gagging Act"**. It was strongly opposed by Indian public opinion.

### Amrita Bazar Patrika
**Amrita Bazar Patrika**, started in **1868** by **Sisir Kumar Ghosh** and **Motilal Ghosh**, was a Bengali paper. To escape the Act, which did not cover English papers, it **turned itself into an English-language newspaper** almost overnight in 1878.

### Repeal
The Act was **repealed in 1882** by **Lord Ripon**, Lytton's successor.

:::keyfacts
- Vernacular Press Act: **1878**, **Lord Lytton**
- Applied **only to vernacular** papers; **no appeal** to courts
- Nickname: **"Gagging Act"**
- Repealed: **1882**, **Lord Ripon**
- Amrita Bazar Patrika: **Bengali → English** (1878)
:::

:::exam-tip
Pair the Viceroys: **Lytton** = Vernacular Press Act and Delhi Durbar of 1877; **Ripon** = repeal of the Act, local self-government and the Hunter Commission.
:::

## Nationalist newspapers {#nationalist-press}

From the 1870s the press became the main tool of the early nationalists. Many leaders of the early Indian National Congress were also editors or writers. Newspapers spread political ideas, criticised official policies, reported Congress sessions and educated readers about economic issues such as the drain of wealth.

| Paper | Founder / editor | Language | Year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sambad Kaumudi | Rammohun Roy | Bengali | 1821 |
| Mirat-ul-Akbar | Rammohun Roy | Persian | 1822 |
| Rast Goftar | Dadabhai Naoroji | Gujarati | 1851 |
| Amrita Bazar Patrika | Sisir Kumar Ghosh and Motilal Ghosh | Bengali, later English | 1868 |
| Bangadarshan | Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay | Bengali | 1872 |
| The Hindu | G. Subramania Iyer and associates | English | 1878 |
| The Bengalee | Surendranath Banerjea (owner-editor from 1879) | English | — |
| Kesari | Bal Gangadhar Tilak (with Agarkar and others) | Marathi | 1881 |
| Mahratta | Bal Gangadhar Tilak (with Agarkar and others) | English | 1881 |
| Swadesamitran | G. Subramania Iyer | Tamil | — |
| Comrade | Mohammad Ali | English | 1911 |
| Al-Hilal | Maulana Abul Kalam Azad | Urdu | 1912 |

### The press and the law
Writing in nationalist papers often led to prosecution. **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** was tried for sedition in **1897** and again in **1908**, when he was sentenced to six years' imprisonment and sent to **Mandalay** (Burma). Each trial made his papers and his cause more popular.

### Why the press mattered
- Papers were read aloud in villages, libraries and meeting places, so their influence reached far beyond the literate few.
- They gave Indians in different regions a shared view of public questions.
- They helped build public opinion against measures such as the Vernacular Press Act and the Indian Universities Act.

:::caution
**Kesari** was the **Marathi** paper and **Mahratta** the **English** paper of Tilak's group. **The Bengalee** is linked with **Surendranath Banerjea**, while **Bangadarshan** is Bankim Chandra's Bengali journal.
:::

## The English-educated middle class and its role {#middle-class}

Western education, the new universities and the press together created a **new middle class**. It included lawyers, teachers, doctors, journalists, clerks and government servants, mostly in the presidency towns of **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras**.

### Features
- Its members shared a **common language (English)** and similar ideas, which allowed people from different regions to communicate and organise at an all-India level.
- They read about **liberty, democracy, rationalism and nationalism** in Western writings and applied these ideas to India's condition.
- The class was **small** and largely **urban**, and most of its members came from upper castes and better-off families.

### Role in Indian society and politics
- **Social and religious reform**: many reformers of the 19th century came from this class (see the topic on social reform movements).
- **Political associations**: it formed early bodies such as the **British Indian Association (1851)** in Calcutta, the **Indian Association (1876)** founded by **Surendranath Banerjea** and **Ananda Mohan Bose**, and finally the **Indian National Congress (1885)**.
- **Economic critique**: writers such as **Dadabhai Naoroji** used Western economic ideas to expose the **drain of wealth** from India.
- **Journalism**: it ran most of the newspapers that shaped public opinion.

### A result the rulers did not intend
The British had hoped that English education would produce loyal clerks and supporters of their rule. Instead, many English-educated Indians became the **leaders of the national movement**. By the end of the 19th century, officials such as **Lord Curzon** viewed the universities with suspicion, which helps explain the Indian Universities Act of 1904.

### Limitations
- Mass literacy remained very low, because the government spent little on primary education.
- Women's education and technical education were neglected.
- A gap grew between the English-educated few and the rest of society.

:::exam-tip
In "assertion–reason" questions, remember that the British aim (clerks and loyal intermediaries) and the actual result (nationalist leadership) were different. That contrast is a frequent test point.
:::
