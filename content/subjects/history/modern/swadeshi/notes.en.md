# Partition of Bengal and the Swadeshi Movement

## Rise of the Extremists {#extremists}

By the end of the 19th century a new group inside the national movement had lost faith in the methods of the early Congress leaders. Textbooks call the older group the **Moderates** and the newer one the **Extremists** (also called militant or assertive nationalists).

### Leading Extremists
- **Lal-Bal-Pal**: **Lala Lajpat Rai** (Punjab), **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** (Maharashtra) and **Bipin Chandra Pal** (Bengal).
- **Aurobindo Ghosh** (Bengal), who developed the idea of **passive resistance**: peaceful but complete non-cooperation with the foreign government.
- Tilak used popular festivals to reach ordinary people: the **Ganapati festival (1893)** and the **Shivaji festival (1895; some books give 1896)**. He ran the papers **Kesari** (Marathi) and **Mahratta** (English). His slogan **"Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it"** became famous.

### Why Extremism grew
- **Limited results of Moderate methods.** Petitions, prayers and speeches had won few concessions from the British.
- **Economic distress.** Famines and plague in the late 1890s, and the nationalist critique of the "drain of wealth", showed the costs of colonial rule.
- **Curzon's policies** (see next section), which many Indians saw as arrogant and anti-Indian.
- **Cultural revival and self-confidence.** The teachings of Swami Vivekananda and Dayananda Saraswati gave pride in India's past.
- **International events.** Japan's victory over Russia in **1905** showed that an Asian country could defeat a European power. Ethiopia's (Abyssinia's) defeat of Italy at Adowa (1896) had a similar effect.
- **Racial discrimination** against Indians at home and in places such as South Africa.

### Moderates and Extremists compared
| Point | Moderates | Extremists |
|---|---|---|
| Period of dominance | 1885–1905 | 1905–1908 (and later) |
| Goal | Reforms and a larger Indian share in administration, within the British Empire | **Swaraj** (self-rule) |
| Methods | Petitions, prayers, memorials, speeches, press | Boycott, swadeshi, national education, passive resistance, mass action |
| Attitude to British rule | Faith in British sense of justice | No such faith; self-reliance (**atma shakti**) |
| Social base | Mainly the urban educated middle class | Sought to reach students, women and the wider public |
| Leading figures | Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Surendranath Banerjea | Tilak, Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh |

:::caution
The labels "Moderate" and "Extremist" are broad textbook terms. Both groups were nationalists. The Moderates' economic critique of British rule prepared the ground for the Extremists.
:::

:::mnemonic
**Lal-Bal-Pal = Punjab, Maharashtra, Bengal**, in the same order: **L**ajpat Rai (Punjab), **B**al Gangadhar Tilak (Maharashtra), Bipin Chandra **P**al (Bengal).
:::

## Lord Curzon's Policies {#curzon}

**Lord Curzon** was Viceroy from **1899 to 1905**. He was an efficient administrator, but many of his measures were seen by educated Indians as attempts to reduce their influence. This deepened nationalist anger even before the partition.

### Measures that angered nationalists
- **Calcutta Corporation Act (1899)**: reduced the number of elected members in the Calcutta Corporation, giving officials more control.
- **Indian Universities Act (1904)**: based on the **Raleigh Commission (1902)**; increased official control over universities. Nationalists saw it as an attempt to curb the educated class.
- **Official Secrets Act (1904)**: tightened the law on official secrecy; nationalists saw it as a curb on the press.
- **Partition of Bengal (1905)**: his most unpopular measure (see next section).

### Other measures (less controversial)
- **Ancient Monuments Preservation Act (1904)** and revival of the Archaeological Survey for the care of historic buildings.
- Agricultural and irrigation inquiries, and the setting up of the **North-West Frontier Province (1901)**.

:::caution
Do not confuse Curzon's measures with earlier ones. The **Vernacular Press Act (1878)** was passed under **Lord Lytton**, and the **Ilbert Bill (1883)** controversy took place under **Lord Ripon**.
:::

:::keyfacts
- Curzon's term: **1899–1905**
- Calcutta Corporation Act: **1899**
- Indian Universities Act: **1904** (after the Raleigh Commission, 1902)
- Partition of Bengal: **1905**
:::

## The Partition of Bengal (1905) {#partition}

The Bengal Presidency, which then included **Bihar and Orissa**, was a very large province with a population of nearly **8 crore (78 million)**. Proposals to reduce its size had been discussed for years, and a plan was made public in **December 1903**.

### The decision
- The partition was **announced in July 1905** by the Government of India under **Lord Curzon**.
- It **came into effect on 16 October 1905**.
- A new province, **Eastern Bengal and Assam**, was formed with its capital at **Dacca** (Dhaka). Its first Lieutenant-Governor was **Bampfylde Fuller**.
- The rest of the province, still called **Bengal**, kept **Calcutta** as its capital and included Bihar and Orissa.

### Two views of the partition
| Official reason | Nationalist view |
|---|---|
| Bengal was too large to be governed efficiently by one Lieutenant-Governor | The real aim was to weaken Bengal, the nerve centre of Indian nationalism |
| A smaller province would mean better administration, especially for the neglected eastern districts | It divided Bengalis on **territorial** lines: in the western province Bengali speakers would be outnumbered by Hindi and Oriya speakers |
| — | It divided them on **religious** lines: the new eastern province had a **Muslim majority** and the western one a **Hindu majority** |

Nationalists, and many later historians, saw the plan as a case of **divide and rule**. Official papers of the time also show that weakening the political influence of educated Bengalis was one of the considerations behind it (for example, a 1904 note by the Home Secretary H.H. Risley argued that a united Bengal was a political force, while a divided Bengal would be pulled in different directions).

:::keyfacts
- Plan made public: **December 1903**
- Announced: **July 1905**
- In force: **16 October 1905**
- New province: **Eastern Bengal and Assam**, capital **Dacca**
- Viceroy: **Lord Curzon**
:::

:::caution
The partition was **announced** in July 1905 but **came into force** on **16 October 1905**. The day of protest was 16 October, not the date of the announcement.
:::

## The Anti-Partition Agitation {#anti-partition}

Protest began as soon as the plan became known in 1903. At first the Moderate leaders used their usual methods: petitions, public meetings, memorials and press campaigns. Leaders such as **Surendranath Banerjea**, **Krishna Kumar Mitra** and **Prithwishchandra Ray** led this stage. When it failed to change the government's mind, the movement turned to new methods.

### Swadeshi and Boycott
- On **7 August 1905**, a large meeting at the **Calcutta Town Hall** passed the **Boycott resolution**. This is taken as the formal start of the **Swadeshi movement**.
- **Boycott** meant refusing to buy British goods, especially Manchester cloth and Liverpool salt. Foreign cloth was burnt in public, and shops selling it were picketed.
- **Swadeshi** ("of one's own country") meant using Indian-made goods instead.
- Boycott later spread to government schools, courts, titles and offices, and to **social boycott** of those who bought foreign goods.

### 16 October 1905
- The day the partition came into force was observed as a **day of mourning** across Bengal. There was a **hartal** (general strike) in Calcutta.
- People bathed in the Ganga and walked the streets singing **"Bande Mataram"**, which became the slogan of the movement.
- On **Rabindranath Tagore's** suggestion, people tied **rakhis** on each other's wrists (**Raksha Bandhan**) as a symbol of the unity of the two halves of Bengal.
- Tagore wrote **"Amar Sonar Bangla"** in this period; it became a song of the movement and is now the **national anthem of Bangladesh**.

### Spread outside Bengal
- **Tilak** in Bombay and Poona, **Lala Lajpat Rai** and **Ajit Singh** in Punjab, and **V.O. Chidambaram Pillai** in the Madras Presidency carried the message of swadeshi to other regions.

### Repression
- The **Carlyle Circular (1905)** threatened to cut grants to schools whose students joined the agitation.
- The **Bengal Provincial Conference at Barisal (April 1906)** was broken up by police with a lathi charge.
- Public singing of "Bande Mataram" was banned in places, and many leaders and newspapers were prosecuted.

:::timeline
- Dec 1903 | Partition plan made public
- Jul 1905 | Partition announced
- 7 Aug 1905 | Boycott resolution at Calcutta Town Hall
- 16 Oct 1905 | Partition in force; day of mourning and Raksha Bandhan
- Apr 1906 | Police break up the Barisal conference
:::

## Constructive Swadeshi: Education, Industry and Culture {#constructive-swadeshi}

The movement was not only a protest. Its leaders stressed **atma shakti** (self-reliance) and tried to build Indian alternatives in education, industry and culture.

### National education
- The **National Council of Education** was set up on **15 August 1906** to organise education "on national lines" and under national control.
- The **Bengal National College** was started in Calcutta in 1906 with **Aurobindo Ghosh** as its principal.
- The **Bengal Technical Institute** (1906) gave technical training; it later grew into **Jadavpur University**.
- Tagore's school at **Shantiniketan** was admired as a model of education in Indian surroundings. National schools were opened in many districts.

### Swadeshi enterprises
- Textile mills, handlooms, soap and match factories, tanneries, banks and insurance companies were started with Indian capital.
- **Acharya Prafulla Chandra Ray's Bengal Chemicals** (founded before the movement) gained from the demand for Indian goods.
- **V.O. Chidambaram Pillai** founded the **Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company** at **Tuticorin** (1906) to compete with British shipping.

### Samitis (volunteer bodies)
- Samitis trained volunteers, spread the message through songs and plays, did social work during famines and enforced boycott.
- The **Swadesh Bandhab Samiti** of **Ashwini Kumar Dutt** in **Barisal** was one of the best known.
- Other groups included the **Anushilan Samiti**, which later took a revolutionary path.

### Culture
- Songs by **Rabindranath Tagore**, **Rajanikanta Sen** and **Dwijendralal Ray**, and the jatras of **Mukunda Das**, carried the message to villages.
- **Abanindranath Tagore** painted **"Bharat Mata"**. Nandalal Bose and others drew on Indian artistic traditions.
- In science, **Jagadish Chandra Bose** and **Prafulla Chandra Ray** became symbols of Indian achievement.

:::keyfacts
- National Council of Education: **15 August 1906**
- Bengal National College principal: **Aurobindo Ghosh**
- Swadesh Bandhab Samiti: **Ashwini Kumar Dutt, Barisal**
- Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company: **V.O. Chidambaram Pillai, Tuticorin**
- "Bharat Mata" painting: **Abanindranath Tagore**
:::

## From Calcutta (1906) to Surat (1907) {#congress-split}

The Swadeshi movement sharpened the differences between Moderates and Extremists inside the Indian National Congress.

### Calcutta session, 1906
- Presided over by **Dadabhai Naoroji**. The Moderates chose him because he was respected by both groups, which avoided an open clash.
- For the first time the Congress declared **"Swaraj"** (self-government, of the kind enjoyed by the self-governing British colonies) as its goal.
- It passed resolutions on **Swadeshi, Boycott, National Education and Self-Government**.

### The Surat split, 1907
- The Extremists wanted the boycott and swadeshi programme extended across India and to all forms of association with the government. The Moderates wanted to limit boycott to Bengal and to foreign goods.
- At the **Surat session (December 1907)** the Moderates put forward **Rash Behari Ghosh** as president. The Extremists wanted **Lala Lajpat Rai**. The session broke up in disorder, and the Congress **split**.
- The Moderates kept control of the Congress, and the Extremists stayed out until the two groups reunited at the **Lucknow session (1916)**.

### After the split
- The government came down hard on the Extremists.
- **Lala Lajpat Rai** and **Ajit Singh** were deported to Mandalay in 1907 during agrarian unrest in Punjab.
- **Tilak** was convicted of sedition in **1908** for articles in *Kesari* and sentenced to **six years** in **Mandalay** (Burma).
- **Aurobindo Ghosh** was tried in the Alipore Bomb Case, acquitted, and later withdrew to **Pondicherry** to devote himself to spiritual life.
- **Bipin Chandra Pal** withdrew from active politics for a while.

:::caution
The **Calcutta session (1906)** adopted the Swaraj goal and the four resolutions; it did **not** split. The split came a year later at **Surat (1907)**. The Surat president was **Rash Behari Ghosh** (a Moderate), not Dadabhai Naoroji.
:::

:::exam-tip
Remember the pairs: **1906 Calcutta — Naoroji — Swaraj**; **1907 Surat — Rash Behari Ghosh — split**; **1916 Lucknow — reunion**.
:::

## The Muslim League and the Morley-Minto Reforms {#league-morley-minto}

### The Muslim League (1906)
- On **1 October 1906**, a delegation of Muslim leaders led by the **Aga Khan** met the Viceroy **Lord Minto** at Simla. This is called the **Simla Deputation**. It asked for separate representation for Muslims in councils and other bodies.
- The **All-India Muslim League** was founded at **Dacca** on **30 December 1906**. Its leading founders included the **Aga Khan**, **Nawab Salimullah of Dacca** and **Nawab Mohsin-ul-Mulk**.
- In its early years the League supported the partition of Bengal, asked for separate electorates and professed loyalty to the British government.

### The Morley-Minto Reforms (Indian Councils Act, 1909)
Named after **John Morley** (Secretary of State for India) and **Lord Minto** (Viceroy), the reforms were meant partly to win over the Moderates and partly to answer the Muslim demand.
- The number of members in the **Imperial (central) and provincial legislative councils** was increased.
- **Elections were indirect.** Members were chosen by bodies such as municipalities, district boards, landholders and chambers of commerce.
- **Separate electorates** were introduced for **Muslims**: some seats were reserved for them, and only Muslim voters could vote for them.
- The **official majority** was kept in the Imperial Legislative Council; provincial councils got a **non-official majority**.
- Members could discuss the budget, move resolutions and ask supplementary questions, but they had no real control over the government.
- **Satyendra Prasanna Sinha** became the **first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council** (Law Member).

### Views on separate electorates
- The Muslim League had asked for them and welcomed them.
- Nationalist leaders criticised them for dividing voters on religious lines. Many textbooks therefore describe **Lord Minto** as the "father of communal electorates".

:::caution
The 1909 Act did **not** introduce **dyarchy** or **direct elections**. Dyarchy in the provinces came with the **Government of India Act, 1919** (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms).
:::

## Annulment of the Partition and Assessment {#annulment-assessment}

### Annulment, 1911
- At the **Delhi Durbar** held on **12 December 1911** in honour of **King George V**, the partition of Bengal was **annulled**. The Viceroy was **Lord Hardinge** (Hardinge II).
- The Bengali-speaking districts were reunited into one province of Bengal.
- **Bihar and Orissa** became a separate province, and **Assam** was again made a separate province (chief commissionership).
- At the same Durbar the **transfer of the capital from Calcutta to Delhi** was announced. The government moved to Delhi in 1912, and **New Delhi** was formally inaugurated as the capital in **1931**.

### Why the movement declined (by about 1908)
- **Severe repression**: arrests, deportations, bans on meetings and prosecutions of newspapers.
- **The Surat split (1907)** weakened the Congress, and the main Extremist leaders were jailed or withdrew.
- **No effective organisation** to carry on a long struggle; a mass movement at such a high pitch was hard to sustain for long.
- The movement **did not reach the peasantry** widely, and largely failed to draw in the **Muslim peasantry of East Bengal**. Historians such as Bipan Chandra also point to official encouragement of communal division.

### Achievements
- It spread nationalism beyond the educated few to **students, women and sections of the urban and rural population**.
- It introduced methods later used by Gandhi: **boycott, swadeshi, passive resistance and constructive work**.
- It gave a strong push to **Indian industry, education, literature, art and science**.
- Historian **Sumit Sarkar** has traced several trends within the movement, ranging from moderate and constructive swadeshi to political extremism and revolutionary activity.

- 1899 | Curzon becomes Viceroy; Calcutta Corporation Act
- 1904 | Indian Universities Act
- 16 Oct 1905 | Partition of Bengal comes into force
- 1 Oct 1906 | Simla Deputation meets Lord Minto
- 30 Dec 1906 | Muslim League founded at Dacca
- Dec 1906 | Calcutta Congress adopts the goal of Swaraj
